## AI engineering and research

Charles Frye (Modal) — "What Is an Inference Engine, Anyway?" — Frye follows a single request through server IO, tokenization, scheduling, model execution and detokenization, using SGLang and vLLM as reference points. He reads a traffic spike off an inference dashboard, then opens up the engine: KV caching, CUDA graphs, speculative decoding, kernel libraries, and why the scheduler can bottleneck a GPU although it does little computation. It ends with correctness and production debugging (evaluate the real deployment, log token IDs to catch tokenizer bugs) and points to small reference engines worth reading. Miguel González Fernández (Browserbase) and Corby Rosset (Microsoft Research) — "Your LLM Judge Is a Confident Liar: Building Better Verifiers" — They report that the LLM judge bundled with a popular web-agent benchmark said agents succeeded 74% of the time, while a better verifier said 38%. Their "Universal Verifier" uses task-specific rubrics, ranks the most relevant screenshots as evidence for each criterion, and separates controllable from uncontrollable failures. By their account it cut false positives from about half to near zero and agreed with humans as often as humans agree with each other (Cohen's kappa). They also test whether an autoresearch loop can rebuild the verifier. This is the authors' own claim, but it is a concrete measurement of benchmark inflation for computer-use agents. Anant Srivastava (Oracle) — "Stop Fine-Tuning to Fix Retrieval Problems" — Srivastava argues that prompt, memory and weights are three tools for three jobs, not a ladder to climb when answers go wrong. Prompt is for small stable behaviour; memory for knowledge that is current, large, citable or access-controlled; weights only for reflexes that have stopped changing. He illustrates with a support assistant that kept inventing product names after a fine-tune. Opinion backed by an anecdote, but a usable diagnostic.
