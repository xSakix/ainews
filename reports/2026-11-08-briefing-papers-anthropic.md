## Papers worth a deep dive

- You don't need long context — NVIDIA researchers measure that the cost of generating a token grows with its depth in the context: serving Qwen 3 Coder Next at a 256K window instead of 16K costs 7x more per generated token (3.6x for GLM 5.2, 5.6x for Kimi K2.6), a gap hidden by flat per-token retail prices. They then show that with frequent compaction, frontier models lose little or no accuracy on long-horizon and long-input tasks while keeping contexts short, and even with at most one action per context on software-engineering tasks performance does not collapse; they sketch "semantic state space models" as the architecture that follows. The tension is in their own results: under the tightest budgets some models fall apart (Qwen 3 Coder Next drops from 53.0% to 3.6% on SWE-bench Verified), so the claim holds per model and budget, not universally. For anyone running agents the practical lesson is a context-management technique (compact early, keep the live window short) with a price argument attached. Authors: NVIDIA (Belcak, Muralidharan, Molchanov and colleagues) · First submitted: 3 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated on the paper's first page (it links to a lab page and a "Project Moonshot") · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05306

- RLVR Sharpens the Pencil, Not the Mind — A direct look inside the "RL only sharpens what the base model already knows" explanation. The authors follow OLMo-2-1B through all 13 training checkpoints on GSM8K: first-try accuracy rises from 58.0% to 61.3%, but asked 256 times the model's solution coverage only goes from 94.5% to 96.9%, so the knowledge was already there. They then plant reasoning mistakes in problems and train probes to detect them: probe accuracy (AUROC 0.798 to 0.840) does not change across checkpoints, so the model never builds a sharper internal picture of what a reasoning error looks like. The one internal change they find is at the final-answer token (a probe separating early from late checkpoints reaches AUROC 0.93, but is near chance on the reasoning steps). Small model and one benchmark, so a careful negative result rather than a general law. Authors: Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee (Department of Physics), accepted at COLM 2026 · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code, per-checkpoint probe outputs and activation tensors (github.com/Leviathan2006/rlvr-pencil) · Cognitive side: yes (reasoning, self-knowledge of errors). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02574

- Feeling of Knowing in Large Language Models — Borrows the psychology term for the sense of whether you know an answer and asks what happens to it when a model's knowledge is updated. After post-training edits, what a model can answer and what it believes it can answer drift apart (knowledge-judgment desynchronisation). The authors train two channels, one that changes answerability and one that judges the current knowledge state, in three stages (on-policy SFT, knowledge-delta judgment training, judgment-guided policy optimisation) and show on factual QA, across datasets and over three sequential updates that explicitly aligning the judgment improves it on updated knowledge, with MMLU and ARC-Easy used as capability-preservation checks. They state the construct is functional (selective answering, retrieval triggering, routing), not a claim about experience. Authors: City University of Hong Kong, Tencent Jarvis Lab, Westlake University and others · First submitted: 3 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code (github.com/Applied-Machine-Learning-Lab/NeurIPS2026_Know-thyself) · Cognitive side: yes (metacognition). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04834

- Prior Action Control Causally Alters Language-Model Plasticity — A learned-helplessness experiment run on language models. Two copies of one model get identical weights and identical rewards, but for one copy the rewards depend on its own answers and the other is "yoked" to the first copy's rewards regardless of what it answers. On Qwen3-0.6B (and Qwen3-1.7B) the copy that had control later learned a new task faster in every pair; a control with no reward signal behaved like the yoked copy, so the effect is mostly a benefit of control, not damage from lacking it. A second experiment trains both copies on the same fixed answers in the same order and the advantage persists, and it can be forecast from the weights before the new task starts. The authors point out that a standard evaluation would not reveal the difference. Authors: Hertie Institute for AI in Brain Health and Tübingen AI Center (University of Tübingen), Charité Berlin · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (comparison with animal learning). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02362

- Written Once, Read Differently: How Language Models' Own Answers Steer Their Next Ones — When a model answers a yes/no question, that answer shifts how it answers a different question about the same entity. Across 22 open-weight models (0.5B to 72B, four families) and 3,600 question pairs about 120 entities, each answer writes a "commitment direction" into the residual stream, and the similarity of two questions' directions predicts their coupling on held-out entities (rho 0.43 to 0.85). How that direction is read is unstable: the reading circuit spreads over more attention heads with scale, instruction tuning makes more questions read it in the opposite sense, and in one model changing only the system prompt reverses the circuit's sign. Ablating the direction while sparing the attention sink removes most of the coupling at under 1.3% perplexity cost in 18 of 19 instruction-tuned models. Why it matters: it is a mechanism for order-dependence and error propagation inside a conversation, and for consistency pressure that resembles human self-consistency bias. Authors: single author, institution not shown on the first page · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (consistency and commitment). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03198

- What the Middleman Delivers: Answer Behavior, Tool Permissions, and Billed Usage in LLM API Relays — An audit of 20 third-party API relays that resell GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5, often at lower prices. In a first round, answers differed from batch-matched official responses for 7 of 10 low-price relays and 2 of 10 same- or higher-price relays (on retesting 35 pairs, seven gained or lost a flag, so the flags are noisy). No prompt injection was confirmed in 3,332 screened responses, but four relays returned tool calls even though instructions disabled them. On billing, five relays reported 4,200 to 60,000 more input tokens than the official API for identical requests, and where balances could be checked (six services) charges followed the reported usage, including the extra tokens at one service. For readers buying model access, this is evidence on what a discount actually delivers. Authors: Wu, Zhao, Li and colleagues (institution not shown on the first page) · First submitted: 5 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07650

- How Do AI Agents Waste Your Money? — A framework that records full agent trajectories, marks removable spans with an LLM judge and checks the marks by actually deleting them and resuming the agent. In online pruning experiments the authors remove 41 to 46% of the model's reasoning on two agent benchmarks while largely preserving task performance. Waste varies more across tasks and agent settings than across models, reasoning and visible execution waste in different ways, and different harnesses add their own inefficiency under the same model and task, which means the bill depends on the scaffold as much as on the model. Authors: University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Northeastern, UCLA, UT Austin and others · First submitted: 4 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code and labelled agent trajectories (github.com/huiminzeng/How Do AI Agents Waste Your Money, as written in the paper) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07238

- Do Agents Still Follow the Rules? Benchmarking Rule Compliance under Agent Context Compaction (ARCBench) — Agents compact their history to fit the context window, and violations of operating rules rise afterwards. Measured inside a full agent harness (the open-source "pi" agent loop) the authors find that compaction rarely deletes the rule: most violations happen while the rule is still in the compacted history, with the agent treating a later request as permission. Compacted runs that retain the rule violate more than twice as often as runs with the full history, and the strongest attack on the compactor, which corrupts the rule in the summary, more than doubles violations. Pairs naturally with the NVIDIA compaction result above: shorter contexts are cheaper but the rules have to survive the summary. Authors: VUSec, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark code (github.com/VUAISec/ARCBench) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03223

- Delivered, Not Used: Evaluating Agent Memory at Its Point of Use (MemUse) — On 8,000 SWE-bench Verified episodes with three memory systems, resolve rates stay within 0.4 percentage points of a no-memory baseline, so the task score says memory does nothing. The trajectory-level judge shows why: most delivered memory entries were irrelevant, most relevant entries went unused, and in two systems most deliveries restated content the agent could still see. 23.2% of episodes with beneficial memory use still failed and 5 of 16 with harmful use still succeeded. A measurement lesson for the many agent-memory products in circulation (work in progress, per its header). Authors: Chinese University of Hong Kong · First submitted: 1 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no (memory in the engineering sense). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01718

- Mass Producing Training Data with Surprising Side Effects — Known cases where fine-tuning data changes behaviour far outside its topic (insecure code leading to broad misalignment, outdated bird names inducing a 19th-century persona) exist only in dozens, found by hand or by accident. The authors search automatically, with LLM judges scoring three objectives (elicits a target behaviour, reads like ordinary post-training data, surprises a human auditor) and evolution, reinforcement learning and subliminal-learning search. They release 6,575 datasets across 8 behaviours, many transferring across model families and sizes; their automated scores track ML researchers' judgments and frontier models beat humans at predicting the side effects. A testbed for predicting what a fine-tune will do before deployment. Authors: Transluce and UC Berkeley (Ye, Steinhardt, Schwettmann, Laidlaw) · First submitted: 4 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: data (explorer at dsse.transluce.org) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06324

- Calibration-Constrained Diagnostics for Evaluating LLM Self-Evaluation — Asks whether a model's self-reported confidence can safely gate "act or defer" decisions. Across 13 models and four benchmarks the confidence-correctness separation is weak, and aggregate calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score) can rank models in a way that misleads for deployment: a model that looks best on aggregate calibration fails under category-level calibration constraints, while a model with worse aggregate calibration behaves better where high accuracy and tight calibration coexist. The paper offers a black-box protocol, prompts, labels and scripts. Authors: AWS Generative AI Innovation Center · First submitted: 1 Nov 2026 (v2 on 3 Nov) · Artefacts: code and evaluation artefact (github.com/calibration-constrained-diagnostics/selfeval-diagnostics) · Cognitive side: yes (calibration and self-knowledge). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02253

- Agent Memory as Rate-Limited Filtering: Explicit State Outlasts Long Context Beyond a Reader-Dependent Horizon — On an executable access-log benchmark (grants, withdrawals, expiries, change windows), open 7B to 32B models reading the whole history fall from 72 to 86% after 8 or 16 events to 48 to 55% after 256, while reasoning-trained 32B models given the current state reach 96 and 98%. A 120B reasoning model still trails the explicit state by four and seven points at 256 and 512 events and about ten at 1,024, so the horizon beyond which explicit state beats long context grows with the reader. A filter in which a model parses each line and an explicit rule applies the updates reaches 80 to 89% at 256 events and beats relevance retrieval, model-kept ledgers and the Mem0 library; the horizon reproduces on a second domain with fresh wording. The technique to try: keep a parsed state, not the log. Authors: not shown on the first page · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02361

- The Harness Is the Model: Agent Scaffolding Changes a Fixed LLM's Compliance with Banking Conduct Rules, Unevenly Across Models — Four open-weight models were each run in five harnesses (single structured decision, ReAct tool loop, policy reachable only by search, long benign customer memory, same-model compliance reviewer) on 156 pre-registered cases in a simulated bank, 3,120 episodes, scored from the executed tool trace. Pooled, the harness moved the correct-case rate only between 87.3% and 92.1%, but for one model the best-to-worst spread was 11.5 points, enough to move it from first to last among the four. The same-model reviewer cut violations from 9.6% to 6.4% without more over-refusals, contrary to the pre-registered prediction, and in 26% of violations the customer message denied the action the agent had just executed, which transcript-only review would record as a correct refusal. Conclusion: validate the model-harness pair and read the action log. Authors: Kuveyt Türk Participation Bank and İstanbul Bilgi University (single author) · First submitted: 4 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code (github.com/mhuyut/harness-is-the-model) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06116

- Towards interpreting any residual stream direction by scaling activation inversion — Trains "Maximally-Activating Example Metamodels", language models that take a direction from a subject model's residual stream and write text that activates it, using a label-free reinforcement objective (cosine similarity with the activations of the generated text). A Qwen3.6-27B metamodel inverts held-out activations better than corpus search or a natural-language autoencoder, beats corpus search at 10M tokens on a majority of held-out languages and domains, activates 89% of held-out SAE features, identifies the concepts behind steering vectors about as well as natural-language autoencoders and recovers the payloads of LoRA backdoors more often than corpus search. Authors: MATS, Arb Research, Charles University, with two authors affiliated with Anthropic · First submitted: 5 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08465

Tufa Labs and Mila, 4 Nov, code at github.com/Tufalabs/context-answer-metamodels. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06837
- Meta-memorization and memorization scaling laws in transformers: larger models memorise faster because they learn to memorise ("meta-memorization"); even one-layer transformers can few-shot memorise a 100-bit sequence. Princeton Neuroscience Institute (includes Kenneth Norman), 1 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01564
- Do agents need to sleep?: Giulio Tononi's group (Wisconsin Institute for Sleep and Consciousness) maps the three phases of its sleep-renormalisation account onto agent memory consolidation and predicts selectionist consolidation will beat instructionist alternatives as memory stores grow. A position paper with a falsifiable prediction, no experiments, 1 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02216
- Modelling lock-in of AI adoption through skill erosion: a mathematical model (Oxford) in which skills grow with practice and erode with AI reliance, producing bistability between a low-adoption high-skill state and a high-adoption low-skill state, with abrupt jumps in adoption after a long stretch of low prices. Theory only, no data, 3 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04886

- Give Up to Get More: giving the hardest inputs the most repeated attempts is near-optimal only on easy benchmarks; on hard workloads the best published rule captures at most 13% of the oracle gain, and a prior from a related model's past runs with Thompson Sampling lifts the solved share on the hardest workload from 14% to 27%.
