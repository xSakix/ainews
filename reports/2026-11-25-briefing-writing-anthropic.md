## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- Returning to the Neuron Basis for Interpretability, Parts 1 and 2 (Anthropic Fellows, 23 November) — Two companion posts argue that individual MLP neurons are more interpretable than the field assumed since sparse autoencoders took over. A neuron adds its activation times one fixed output vector to the residual stream, so each can be read as a context-gated steering vector. Part 1 revisits three standard objections. A neuron contributes two unrelated concepts at once in about 1% or fewer of sampled cases. Its output keeps roughly the same meaning across most layers. Facts are spread over many neurons, but those neurons generally represent concepts related to the fact. Part 2 tests usefulness on open-weight models. Neurons chosen from descriptions and readouts match trained steering vectors, and both trigger the target behaviour in 21% of responses. As zero-shot safety classifiers they match linear probes trained on 400 labels on 4 of 16 evaluation sets, but are usually worse than classifiers trained on full labelled data. In a blind audit of 68 LoRA adapters (58 with a hidden behaviour), a Claude auditor reading neurons names the behaviour in 75% of audits against 40% from outputs alone. Of 23 WMDP-cyber unlearning checkpoints, 14 still rank the correct answer first under cloze scoring on most questions the base model got right, which suggests many unlearning methods hide knowledge instead of removing it. Caveats: Fellows-programme work on open-weight models (the worked example is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), the authors compare against baselines they chose, and the audit used model organisms with planted behaviours. Source: https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/ and https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-applications/
Author: Jasmine Shone and Lisa Dunlap, Anthropic Fellows · Format: deep dive (two parts) · Length: about 30 minutes for both parts

- Skip the DRAM Tier: running the agentic KV cache on NVMe with vLLM and MoRI UMBP (23 November) — The vLLM team asks whether the KV-cache pool for multi-turn agent sessions needs a DRAM tier. On AMD Instinct MI355X, a 1.8 TB NVMe pool matched a 1.8 TB DRAM pool within 1% on Kimi-K3 at 48, 70 and 96 concurrent sessions (for example 13,380 vs 13,492 tok/s per GPU at 70 sessions), in two sets of runs on different machines. The mechanism is the cache hit rate: in 42 runs, 1 percentage point more of prompt tokens served from cache was worth about 10% more throughput, and same-size pools that evict the same way keep the same KV. The economics are the interesting part. In the AgentX replay of real Claude Code sessions the median turn is 142K input tokens and over 96% of them are a repeated prefix. Kimi-K3 wrote 2.3 to 4.6 TB of KV per hour, so a 1.8 TB DRAM pool (about $76,000 at list price) holds only 23 to 47 minutes of it. Drives rated for the 83 TB per day of writes hold a full day for about $59,000. Caveats: written with AMD's library, on AMD hardware, at list prices; every KV block is written to the drives, so wear is the stated limitation and a distributed pool is only on the roadmap; one model and one benchmark. It also notes that DeepSeek already keeps agent KV on SSD. Source: https://blog.vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-23-skip-the-dram-tier
Author: vLLM team with AMD and Moonshot AI collaborators (inference engineers) · Format: deep dive · Length: about 35 minutes

- From CUDA and JAX to TorchTPU: lessons from a Cayley graph solver (24 November) — A first-hand port of a beam-search puzzle solver (CayleyPy Professor Tetraminx, a 3.4M-parameter transformer heuristic over 51 tokens) to Google's new native PyTorch TPU backend. Getting it to run was easy and running it well was not. The first training port was 1.65x slower than the A100 pipeline and the first beam search 5.7x slower than the JAX version. After fixing eager execution, rewriting attention, removing dynamic shapes and a deduplication bug, training reached roughly A100 wall-clock and TorchTPU beam search became 25% faster per level than JAX on the same eight-chip v6e machine, with identical total solution length on six test puzzles. An upstream TorchTPU issue led to attention-routing fixes. Useful as a concrete log of what TPU porting costs. Caveats: one task, one author, and the code and profiling were done by Claude Code and Codex under the author's direction (the author says so). The notebooks run on free Kaggle TPU v5e-8. Hacker News discussion: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633424 (72 points, 1 comment at time of reading). Source: https://andlukyane.com/blog/cayley-graphs-and-torchtpu
Author: Andrey Lukyanenko, ML practitioner · Format: deep dive · Length: about 20 minutes

- Import AI 482 — SciResearch-Eval and AscendGYM (23 November) — Jack Clark's newsletter summarises two papers. SciResearch-Eval (Lawrence Livermore) gives an agent a heat-conduction measurement task with a simulator that may be under-resolved or physically wrong, and scores 95% intervals against ground truth. When the simulator lacks real physics, only GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 are reliable at default settings, the Claude 5.5 models recover only at their highest reasoning setting, and older and open-weight models do not recover, per the authors as quoted. This is a calibration and metacognition result in the profile's sense. AscendGYM is a kernel-optimisation benchmark for Huawei Ascend chips, where Opus 5 scored best of the models tested, with Claude Code the best harness. The RSI and export-control interpretation is Clark's. Source: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-482-huawei-kernel-benchmarks

## Essays and arguments

- Three LLM inflection points in twelve months (Simon Willison, 24 November) — Willison, on the first anniversary of Claude Opus 4.5, says coding agents crossed three thresholds. Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.1-Codex-Max in November 2025 made the default result usually right. "Fable class" models in June and July 2026 (Claude Fable 5, 5.1, 5.2, Opus 5.5 as "cheap Fable", GPT-6 Astra, perhaps Gemini 4 Neon) are now his baseline. In September and October frontier models became good at 3D modelling and game design: Opus 5.5 spent 12 hours playtesting and rebalancing his RTS after he told it the game was not fun enough. He is unsure whether Fable 5.2 is a fourth inflection, and says he now rarely types code by hand. It is one experienced practitioner's account, built on his own projects (Datasette, LLM) and a conference keynote, and it offers no measurement. Worth reading as a dated timeline. It also records the claim that the indie games scene is in a "culture war" over AI assistance, which is his observation. Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/24/llm-inflection-points/

Humphries expects a milder "Deep Blue" moment for history than for mathematics, because historians reinterpret the past and do not chase a single truth. - Using AI without losing your humanity (Drew Breunig, 24 November) — Breunig argues that labs are trading output diversity for reliability, turning models "from infrastructure into appliances", and that this produces a default aesthetic. His small test: the same prompt ("Make me a website landing page for a B2B CRM company") in fresh sessions returned almost the same page every time, with the same tagline idea, font and colours (dark themes appeared only in late-evening sessions because the model noticed the system time). He sets this against Taylor and Gilbreth's time-and-motion standardisation, which scaled output but removed the weirdness that produced ideas. The prompt test is anecdotal (one prompt, one agent, no counts) and the historical analogy is argument. Practical takeaway: weak short prompts return the default, so vary the brief.
