## 1. Jobs, skills and professions

- Federal Reserve Vice Chair Jefferson: AI is "not truly everywhere", and the productivity upswing predates chatbots — In a 13 November speech Jefferson cites surveys in which 40 to 50 percent of working adults use generative AI at work, but only a modest fraction use it every day. The Census Bureau's firm survey shows roughly 25 percent of US firms using AI in at least one business function, about 40 percent in professional services and information, and about 10 percent in mining and in leisure and hospitality. He says productivity growth has risen in recent years but began before the 2022 chatbots, so "it's too early to say" what AI contributed. He frames this as the early part of a long adoption cycle, like computers after Solow's 1987 remark. Who is affected: workers and firms outside knowledge sectors; monetary policy · Evidence: official speech citing a Census Bureau firm survey and worker surveys · Tension: usage is widespread (40-50% of workers) but daily use and firm-level integration are low, and the productivity gain cannot yet be attributed to AI. Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/jefferson20261113a.htm

- Fed Chairman Warsh opens a Productivity and Jobs Task Force conference with three scenarios for "super intelligence" — At the conference, hosted by the Board and the San Francisco Fed on 16 November, Warsh calls the technology a general-purpose one and sketches outcomes. In the good case, productivity gains are shared and the US stays near full employment. In the second, some jobs are displaced rather than complemented, and the jobless rate rises above levels consistent with full employment even though GDP growth stays above trend. In the third, returns disappoint after heavy investment, equity valuations fall, and unemployment rises with no productivity gain. He says these are not predictions. This is the first Fed-chair-level text to put labour displacement and an investment bust side by side. Who is affected: workers in occupations that could be displaced; US monetary policy · Evidence: official speech (scenarios, no new data) · Tension: the Fed Chairman says the marginal cost of intelligence is "trending toward zero" while the same speech allows for rising unemployment and a valuation fall. Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20261116a.htm

- Stanford AI Index update: corporate AI investment passes $1 trillion in 2026 while employment of young workers in AI-exposed jobs keeps falling — Stanford HAI's AI Index team (15 November) reports global corporate AI investment above $1 trillion so far in 2026, 46 percent more than all of 2025. In every country surveyed, more people expect AI to eliminate jobs than create them, and 60 percent of US adults would be uncomfortable with a new data center nearby. The HAI Digital Economy Lab's indicators show declines among younger workers in AI-exposed occupations continuing into 2026, especially in customer service. A new finding is that the declines have deepened even in occupations where AI supports rather than replaces people, such as marketing and IT managers. The authors say the pattern does not establish causality. The earlier Lab post (21 October) put the drop in AI-exposed jobs at over 4 percent this year. Who is affected: younger workers in customer service, marketing and IT management · Evidence: study and dashboard (Stanford AI Index/HAI Digital Economy Lab), survey data; causality explicitly not established · Tension: record investment against shrinking entry-level employment in the exposed jobs, and public expectation of net job loss. Source: https://hai.stanford.edu/news/the-jagged-edge-of-the-ai-boom

- Fed Governor Barr on AI and inequality: returns may go to capital, not labour — On 12 November Barr argues from research on the falling labour share of income that AI could be "capital-biased innovation". He links this to the dominance of a few hyperscalers and frontier labs, "superstar firms", and the scale of AI capital spending. The open questions he names are how far AI substitutes for rather than complements workers, and how many new jobs it creates. This is a framing speech, with no new measurement. Who is affected: wage earners; the distribution of AI gains · Evidence: official speech citing economic research · Tension: a promised broad productivity gain against a capital-heavy buildout whose returns may not reach workers. Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20261112a.htm

- NBER working paper: will AI growth reach the developing world? — Björkegren, Lagakos and VanVuren (16 November) build a growth model in which AI services trade on world markets and substitute for or complement different labour. Developing countries may gain less because they lack the complementary skills and may be priced out of world markets by low productivity. They simulate making workers complement AI, compute subsidies, faster diffusion and cash transfers, and conclude the risk of falling behind, or of some workers losing in absolute terms, is large enough to study as it happens. The abstract reports no numerical estimates, and the paper is a model, not an observation. Who is affected: workers and governments in lower-income countries · Evidence: study (theoretical model, NBER working paper, not peer reviewed) · Tension: none stated by the sources beyond the model's own mechanisms. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36035

- NBER working paper: first wave of a panel survey of undergraduates on AI — Sacerdote and Parris-Bacon (16 November) survey Dartmouth students, graduating high school seniors and US college students. Students report using AI both for tasks likely to harm learning and for tasks likely to help it, and report far more misuse by peers than by themselves. 92 percent agree AI use is harmful to writing, and graded out-of-class assignments are seen as less fair because of AI-enabled cheating. Among Dartmouth respondents, 84 percent say AI is changing the nature of jobs in their desired field rather than eliminating them. Students are mostly self-taught in AI and want it built into the curriculum. This is a pipeline question for skills: the next cohort of juniors is forming its habits now. Who is affected: undergraduates and the employers who will hire them · Evidence: survey study (self-reported; first wave; NBER working paper) · Tension: students say AI harms writing yet use it heavily and expect it to change, not remove, their jobs. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36021

## 2. How teams work with agents

- Claude Code team on verification: about 90 percent of its code is written and reviewed by Claude — In a 16 November engineering post (Addy Osmani, on claude.dev), the team says that at that share of agent-written code the limit on how many agents one person can run is how well each can verify its work with evidence. Without verification loops, they say, you can run one or two Claudes at a time; with them, dozens or hundreds. The post recommends end-to-end tests first, then property tests (invariants such as "balance never negative", round-trips, idempotency) and differential tests, and warns that flaky or slow tests teach the agent to re-run rather than fix. In the team's internal testing on structured file generation, a separate grader checking against a rubric improved task success by up to 10 points over a standard prompting loop. These are the vendor's own figures from internal tests. Who is affected: engineering teams delegating coding to agents · Evidence: company account (Anthropic's own team; internal testing, no published data) · Tension: none. Source: https://claude.dev/blog/how-the-claude-code-team-thinks-about-verification/

- BIS working paper: a central bank's agentic econometrics assistant with every choice logged — BIS Working Paper 1395 (13 November) presents MaRCo@BIS, a set of plain-text workflow files, a verified dictionary of model specifications and a tested reference library that an off-the-shelf agentic coding tool reads. The agent proposes specifications with their economic rationale, retrieves and harmonises data, estimates, checks results against economic priors and logs every choice for replication, while the researcher confirms or overrides each consequential decision. The authors benchmark the agent's replication of three structural VARs against the originals, run five exercises, and discuss automation bias and specification-search risks. Code is released publicly. Who is affected: economists and analysts at central banks and research institutions · Evidence: working paper with released code · Tension: the paper itself pairs the productivity gain with an explicit warning about automation bias and the need for human supervision. Source: https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1395-marcobis-ai-agentic-econometric-analysis-central-bankers
