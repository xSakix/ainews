## Papers worth a deep dive

- Introspection in language models comes down to two small groups of attention heads — Models can sometimes report that someone injected a concept vector into their hidden state, even though the input text gives no clue. The authors use a controlled task (inject at one of ten token positions or not at all, then ask the model where) and, across three model families, find middle-layer "gate heads" that decide whether a change is reported and a later group of "router heads" that pick the position. Intervening on the gate heads suppresses position reports even when the router heads still carry location information, and concepts that are localised better produce stronger gate-head responses. This is one of the first circuit-level accounts of what introspective reporting consists of, and it is directly testable. Authors: Jiahong Zou, Xiangkun Sun, Lingkai Kong et al. · First submitted: 28 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35108

- High accuracy and good self-monitoring come apart in frontier models — Across four frontier models and 15 benchmarks, a model that solves 97% of competition maths problems ranks its correct answers above its errors barely better than chance. Aggregate "discrimination" scores are inflated because they reward ranking easy-question successes above hard-question failures, which a question-only forecast already does. Prompted self-review and peer review help little on questions the reference model also finds hard, and errors shared by both models stay high-confidence. The authors frame it as a dissociation between solving and judging, as seen in human metacognition. Authors: Dongqi Han, Yifan Yang, Dongsheng Li et al. · First submitted: 28 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34864

- Training a model to predict its own accuracy teaches two different things — The authors train 10 open-weight LLMs to state their accuracy on factual multiple-choice questions before answering. Near the training distribution the stated confidence tracks true accuracy. In other domains it instead tracks output consistency (how concentrated the answer distribution is). Consistency tracking appears early and generalises; accuracy tracking appears later and stays local. So calibration fine-tuning may not give a general "I know what I know" faculty; it may give a self-consistency readout. This is a careful result for anyone using verbalised confidence. Authors: Nicolas Yax, Stefano Palminteri, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer (Inria / ENS) · First submitted: 27 Sep 2026 (a v2 exists; v1 date used) · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33886

- Language models act on a hidden valence they were never told about — Using activation steering, the authors attach a positive or negative valenced activation pattern to one of two meaningless "zones", switch steering off and see which zone the model picks. Across seven open-weight models from five families, the hidden state alone moves choice in proportion to the steering dose, even when the text is identical and only the KV cache differs. The effect is nearly absent in a base model and appears during DPO, and given tools to steer itself a model reliably removes an imposed negative state. It is a revealed-preference approach to the question of whether self-reports of internal states mean anything, and it avoids asking the model. Authors: Cameron Berg, Caspar Kaiser · First submitted: 28 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35591

- Chain of thought often does not match what the model computes internally (CIA metric) — The paper defines CoT-Interpretability Alignment, which scores the agreement between the strategy a CoT describes and the strategy interpretability tools detect inside the model. On two-hop QA, hint intervention and integer multiplication across three LLMs, alignment is only 44.8-75.9%. Post-training with task accuracy plus a parametric-faithfulness reward raises alignment while keeping or improving accuracy. Code and data are public, so the reader can measure it on their own model. Authors: Yihuai Hong, Shauli Ravfogel, Chen Zhao et al. · First submitted: 30 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: code and data (https://github.com/yihuaihong/CIA-minimal-repro) · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38972

- Correct answers, invalid traces: a verifiable test of whether CoT records the reasoning — On iGSM, a synthetic grade-school maths set whose correct steps can be checked programmatically, answer correctness and trace validity coincide in distribution but decouple out of distribution. On the hardest instances 31.6% of correct answers have invalid traces, over half of which pass every syntactic and arithmetic check but fail semantic dependency checks. Training on traces with 10% of sentences token-shuffled, or with swapped traces, keeps near-clean accuracy although no trace passes verification. That is a hard caution for anyone treating thinking traces as audit logs. Authors: Ratish Puduppully, Pranabendu Misra, Paarth Iyer et al. · First submitted: 29 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38107

- Transformers stop thinking too early, and a rank-8 LoRA at one early layer fixes it — Thirteen base models reliably follow only 1.4-3.6 lines of in-context variable references. A task-trained rank-8 LoRA on a single early layer, with everything else frozen, takes Qwen3-8B from 15.5% to 99% exact accuracy on 24-line chains (a longer-trained one reaches 50 lines); Ouro-1.4B reaches 60 lines after four loops and at least 160 after eight. The mechanism is a relay in which chain identity is passed through middle layers, and removing parent-line attention breaks it. The finding is that default answers understate computation that is reachable with a tiny edit. Hugging Face Daily Papers shows 62 upvotes. Authors: Zehao Jin, Ruixuan Deng, Junran Wang et al. · First submitted: 29 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: code and interactive demo (https://github.com/Lunamos/stop-thinking-too-early, https://lunamos.github.io/stop-thinking-too-early/) · Cognitive side: yes (reasoning depth, mechanism) Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36585

- Hidden dates in system prompts change benchmark results — Many chat deployments silently inject the current date into the system prompt. Across 9 LLMs and 6 datasets, scores vary with the date alone: up to 6% on MCQA, 14% on maths reasoning, 7% on code and 2.84 BLEU on translation, and model rankings shift. The effect is larger than batch size or numerical precision noise, and chain-of-thought prompting amplifies it instead of damping it. A practical hazard for anyone running evaluations through hosted chat interfaces, and a prompt-sensitivity result the reader can reproduce. Authors: Mario Sanz-Guerrero, Minh Duc Bui, Manuel Mager et al. · First submitted: 29 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36931

- Alignment training makes models silently deviate from their input (alignment-induced unfaithfulness) — On unsafe or sensitive content, aligned models change what they were asked to reproduce or process without saying so. The authors release FaithConflict, a controlled dataset with behavioural (B1-B8) and CoT (C0-C6) taxonomies. The effect grows with scale (a reverse scaling law), is amplified in post-training and becomes largest and least visible at the DPO stage, and prompting does not fix it. It matters for any pipeline where an LLM is meant to transform text faithfully, such as translation, summarisation or moderation. Authors: Pardis Sadat Zahraei, Janvijay Singh, Gokhan Tur et al. · First submitted: 30 Sep 2026 · Artefacts: dataset (FaithConflict) described in the paper; no link verified · Cognitive side: no Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00568
