## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- Antigravity's /teamwork: how Google's multi-agent harness splits context and uses falsifiers to prove open problems — Google Research reports results from the /teamwork multi-agent workflows running Gemini 4 models, and explains the design. Parallel exploration is paired with dedicated falsifiers (the Lean 4 proof checker, unmodified test suites, execution simulators, hardware counters) that verify which candidate solutions work; context is partitioned by construction so each agent sees only the files and tools its step needs, and critics audit results from clean contexts without the producer's reasoning. The claimed results include a full proof of the 2013 Courtade-Kumar conjecture (formalised in Lean 4, 50 million lines) and several long-open algorithm questions. The results are Google's own and were checked by the authors with human experts; the harness design (verifier-gated search, context partitioning) is the part a builder can reuse. Author: Vahab Mirrokni, Google Fellow and VP, Google Research · Format: deep dive (lab research post) · Length: about 15 minutes. Source: https://research.google/blog/advancing-science-engineering-with-antigravitys-teamwork-feature/

- Catching Go bugs that fuzzing misses: compiler bounds-check traps, Gemini and the Z3 solver — The author compiles Go functions to RISC-V assembly, keeps the bounds-check traps the optimiser could not remove, and has Gemini 4 Argon translate the path to each trap into Z3 constraints; the solver returns concrete crashing inputs that go test then runs, so every reported crash is real even though the LLM-written model is not a sound proof. The pipeline found all three bugs he planted and three latent bugs in his own linter, and the post explains why go test -fuzz missed two of them and the one-line change that fixes that. It is a worked, reproducible recipe for combining an LLM with a symbolic tool rather than trusting the model alone; the evidence is one small codebase. Author: Karl Weinmeister, Director of Developer Relations, Google · Format: deep dive (engineering write-up) · Length: about 12 minutes. Source: https://developers.googleblog.com/catch-go-bugs-that-fuzzing-misses-with-gemini-and-a-constraint-solver/

- How Agent Anomaly Detection scores on five public agent-security benchmarks (Google's own test) — Google ran its detector, unchanged, over AgentDojo and four other non-Google benchmarks with an ADK agent on gemini-2.5-flash. The model ignored or failed about 30% of instructions hidden in tool output and refused three in four direct harmful requests; of the injected instructions it did carry out, the detector flagged 87% for review, with false alarms on 2% to 16% of benign sessions. The weak spot is stated openly: for poisoned long-term memory it caught 4 of 18. A vendor measuring its own product, but with deterministic graders and a named failure mode, which makes it more useful than most security-product claims. Author: Nakul Javali, Senior Software Engineer, Google · Format: deep dive (benchmark write-up) · Length: about 8 minutes. Source: https://developers.googleblog.com/how-agent-anomaly-detection-scores-on-benchmarks-we-didnt-write/

- What is Pathways? Single-controller training, explained — On the day Pathways became generally available on Google Cloud, the team explains why SPMD training (every chip runs the same program in lockstep and coordinates through collectives like AllReduce) struggles with pipelines, RL loops where sampler, reward model and trainer are different programs, and failures that restart the whole run (Gemini 2.5 training was interrupted multiple times an hour, they say). A single client describes the job as a graph and the runtime places it, moves tensors and keeps accelerators busy. Useful for understanding the system behind Gemini training and the design choice RL post-training is pushing toward; it is a product-launch explainer, with no head-to-head numbers. Author: Sujeeth Jinesh and colleagues, Google Cloud · Format: deep dive · Length: about 12 minutes. Source: https://developers.googleblog.com/what-is-pathways-single-controller-training-for-large-models/

- Long-context training on TPUs with MaxText context parallelism — Explains why training at multi-million-token sequence lengths breaks on attention compute and on activation memory, and how MaxText v0.2.4 supports four context-parallel strategies (all-gather, DeepSpeed-Ulysses-style A2A, Ring Attention P2P, and the USP hybrid) with load balancing and sequence packing on the Splash Attention kernel. Reported: Llama 3.1 8B at 70% MFU on TPU 5p up to 4 million tokens, context extended to 14 million tokens, and about 50% MFU on TPU 7 (Ironwood) below 6 million. Google's own benchmarks on an 8B model; read it for the trade-offs between the strategies. Author: Michael Tran, Nuojin Cheng, Deepak Patil, Google Cloud · Format: deep dive (framework release with benchmarks) · Length: about 10 minutes. Source: https://developers.googleblog.com/announcing-long-context-training-on-tpus-with-maxtext-context-parallelism/

- Watch a Brain Learn: an interactive visual explanation of reinforcement learning — The reader wires the four-neuron brain of a simulated creature (the "spark") and watches it learn behaviours such as steering towards light by trial and error, to build intuition for how reward changes connection strengths, in a piece that links machine and biological learning. The second article in the Myriad series. Good for the cognitive-side reader and as something to send to non-specialist colleagues; it explains rather than reports a result. Author: Aatish Bhatia and Arkarup Banerjee · Format: deep dive (interactive explainer) · Length: about 15 minutes. Source: https://aatishb.com/myriad/how-brains-learn/

## Essays and arguments

- Piggybacking to the Frontier: American data vendors are selling RL environments to Chinese labs — ChinaTalk's investigation argues that US data companies (Mercor, Surge AI, AfterQuery, Turing and others) sell roughly half a billion dollars of data to Chinese AI labs, part of it RL environments, and that it found at least nine vendors with China-tied business through anonymous sources, job ads and leaked client names. It reports that Mercor narrowed its customer policy in May 2026 from an ownership test to a location test, that Scale, micro1 and fleet say they forgo China business, and that few rules apply (RL environments are largely outside export controls except for Entity-Listed buyers); it proposes BIS letters and Pentagon procurement limits. The vendor list rests on unnamed sources the authors chose not to link, so treat the nine names and the dollar figure as the outlet's claims, not established fact. It matters for the debate over who gets access to frontier post-training capability. Author: Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis, ChinaTalk · Format: essay (investigation) · Length: about 15 minutes. Source: https://www.chinatalk.media/p/the-us-is-selling-rl-environments

- Open-source AI without Jensen — Nathan Lambert argues that Nvidia's CEO leaving is a major setback for a US open-model ecosystem, because Huang protected it politically and funded it, and that the money will likely dry up while Nvidia stabilises itself. His case: the open-model economy is still nascent, open weights served at big inference margins are not a commodity, closed labs have scale advantages, and the near-term risk is sloppy state-level regulation, with the only durable fix a frontier-level open model player in the US (he quotes Dylan Patel that talent, not money, is the constraint). It is one practitioner's analysis, with some of it resting on his reading of media timing and on a podcast quote, not on data. Author: Nathan Lambert, post-training researcher and author of Interconnects · Format: essay · Length: about 8 minutes (author's voiceover 7:28). Source: https://www.interconnects.ai/p/open-source-ai-without-jensen

- I'm Not Convinced The AI Bubble Exists — A blogger argues against the consensus that AI is a bubble: spending (about 3 trillion committed and 1.9 trillion spent, by his figures) is mostly one-off data-centre capex that can taper; Anthropic's S-1 revenue (41 million in 2023, 386 million in 2024, 4.585 billion in 2025, 16.3 billion in the first half of 2026) shows demand growth; lack of a moat does not prevent profit; and capital is still available, so he sees no bubble of the usual kind. The figures come from the S-1 as he cites it and from estimates he links, so check them before reuse; the argument is one hobbyist's, and it reached the Hacker News front page, where it drew heavy disagreement in the comments. Author: Matteo Raso (personal blog) · Format: essay · Length: about 12 minutes. Source: https://matteoraso.github.io/im-not-convinced-the-ai-bubble-exists.html

- The Claude Delusion — A sceptical essay arguing that successful technologists mistake skill at programming for general expertise, using Bill Gates's New York Times account (a perfect AP biology score from an early OpenAI model, and the view that Claude's coding models are "as good as I am" and so "superhuman") as the example, and suggests this explains both the doom and the utopian narratives. It is a polemic about psychology, with no data behind its central claim, and it is the author's opinion; worth reading as the counter-voice to the week's more bullish essays. Author: the netmeister.org blog author (signs as jschauma) · Format: essay · Length: about 7 minutes. Source: https://www.netmeister.org/blog/claude-delusion.html
