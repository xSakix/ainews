## Papers worth a deep dive

- Video world models fail a "flip test" of world state — FlipTest takes two nearly identical frames whose true futures differ (a box resting millimetres before or past a table edge) and asks whether a video model's predicted future changes across that boundary. All 11 video world models tested fall short: the best averages 18.3% pair accuracy over real and simulated scenes, below the 25% of random guessing, and the models change their prediction as often on steps that leave the future unchanged as on steps that change it. A vision-language model on the same frames is selective, and a coding agent that measures the image comes close to the true boundary, so the deciding information is in the frame and the video models miss it. The authors argue that world modelling should identify the causal states that control the dynamics, not only draw plausible next frames. This is a clean evaluation with a clear tension: the field's "world model" claim against a test that a language-and-vision model passes and the video models do not. Authors: affiliations not in arXiv metadata (Mi Luo, Lijun Yu, Adams Wei Yu, Ming-Hsuan Yang and others; project page flip-test.github.io) · First submitted: 15 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark and project page (code/data release not verified) · Cognitive side: yes (does a model represent the state of the world). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19822

- Neuron communities: small reusable groups of existing neurons that carry specific computations — The method builds a graph linking neurons that are repeatedly attributed together across ten million prompts to Llama-3.1-8B, then runs community detection. Thousands of candidate communities result, and held-out causal tests show groups of up to 128 neurons that are necessary and sufficient within the layers they span for grammatical agreement, token-probability calibration, fixed phrases, in-context copying, addition and multiplication. Coverage is modest and honestly stated: 10.3% of held-out prompts have at least one layer explained on a neuron-sparse basis, 8.7% for four or more layers, 3.3% for twelve or more. It is a way to find reusable units inside an MLP without a learned dictionary, which makes it a direct alternative to sparse-autoencoder features. Authors: affiliations not in arXiv metadata (Igor Shilov, Thomas Fel, Tal Haklay, Lucius Bushnaq, Owen Lewis and others) · First submitted: 17 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in the abstract · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability of what happens inside the model while it computes). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23112

- Grokking can be steered: the learning rule decides how many neurons implement the same algorithm — On modular addition, every learning rule that groks recovers the same Fourier-based algorithm, yet the circuits that implement it differ in size. A neuron stays in use when its credit is large relative to the non-gradient term of its update, a single condition that unifies weight decay, Adam's epsilon and a per-neuron gain decay. Equalising credit across neurons expands the effective circuit from 60% to 99% of the layer's neurons at similar accuracy; the per-neuron normalising optimiser Nero distributes the computation, while Adam, RMSProp, Muon, Shampoo and four others keep small, concentrated circuits. Distributed circuits survive neuron deletion better; concentrated circuits prune further and transfer about twice as fast to a task that recombines the same features. The finding is that the optimiser, not only the data, shapes what an interpretability researcher will find inside a network. Authors: Pascal Sager, Benjamin Grewe, Pau Vilimelis Aceituno, Thilo Stadelmann (affiliations not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 14 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in the abstract · Cognitive side: yes (memorisation to generalisation, circuit structure). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19477

- Do agents still need to be told how to self-improve? — A frozen LLM agent is given a compute budget, tools to collect experience and to update text documents, a general goal, and no externally imposed improvement loop, with gameplay as the testbed. Sufficiently capable models do self-improve, and the strategies they invent match classic cognitive frameworks for self-regulated learning and self-generated curricula in the ML literature. The text artefacts they write transfer to other game horizons, mechanics and 3D navigation. The result matters for the practice of agent design because it removes the hand-built self-improvement scaffold, and it is a cognitive-side result because the agent chooses what to practise and how to record what it learns. Authors: Shobhita Sundaram, Cansu Sancaktar, Phillip Isola (MIT, per the project page) · First submitted: 17 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: project page at ssundaram21.github.io/self-org; code not verified · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23219

- LLM trading agents earn beta, not alpha — rta-bench-1 replays headlines only after their publication minute, plays each period blind and named, checks every run for look-ahead, and splits returns into factor exposure and alpha. Two runs of the same model that differed only in the random seed ended 5.2 points apart on average. Across 91 runs of six models from four vendors, no alpha was significant, market and style exposure explained a median 78% of return, and decisions did no better than random stocks of the same sector. The agents bought the stocks with the most positive news, yet in ablations earned as much without any headlines. The agents did follow rules reliably: one cash rule in the prompt cut a GPT-based agent's stock share by 30.3 points in all six pairs. This is a measured gap between "agents that trade for users" and what they actually deliver, with a method that other benchmarks can reuse. Authors: Jackson Wang (single author; affiliation not in metadata) · First submitted: 14 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code and frozen markets stated in the paper comment · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19530

- Most "novel" open-weight LLM architectures are borrowed, and few novelty claims are tested — The author reads the Hugging Face transformers reference code and 40 randomly sampled technical reports. Of the 48 architectures added to the library in 2025, 39 take at least two of their three core blocks from another model, most often Llama (partly an artefact of how the library writes code). In the reports, only 19 of 73 architectural or training novelty claims come with a controlled with/without comparison; 4 of 40 state more than one training run; 8 rerun the models they compare against under one setup; and 1 discloses data mixture, token count and compute together. None of five suspected renamed components hid its origin. Seven reporting rules are proposed. It is a useful lens for reading every technical report that lands on the releases desk. Authors: Bahaeddin Türkoğlu (single author) · First submitted: 15 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: original dataset stated in the paper comment · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20003

- Weight-space programming does not need Transformers — Three frontier LLMs are given ten labelled examples and a sandbox and asked to write a network's weights by hand, with no gradient training, either for a fixed architecture or by designing the architecture themselves. Free to design, all three compile plain-text facts into a sparse lookup table queried by a fixed procedure, reaching up to 54.4% test accuracy on mostly multiple-choice questions. An LLM-designed table model beats the most accurate Transformer the same LLM wrote, with about 1,800 times fewer parameters, and each wrong answer traces to one row the LLM can find and rewrite. The authors derive an associative-memory model up to 17 times faster on GPUs than a same-sized Transformer. Pretrained LLMs remain more accurate; the claim is about which structure to build when a model writes weights. A companion benchmark, WeightBench (2611.18553, 13 Nov), formalises the task with 42 tasks from 2K-parameter policies to 7B models. Authors: Hyunjong Ok, Jaeho Lee (affiliations not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 17 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated; marked work in progress · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22055

- Linear probes can be "pretty liars": unregularised probes pick features the model does not use — Decodability of a feature does not mean the network uses it. The paper shows theoretically that ordinary least squares probes amplify low-variance directions with spurious correlations to the target, while ridge regularisation reduces that. In a toy transformer and in Llama-2-7B, intervention faithfulness rises with regularisation strength up to a point and then falls, and at the optimum the probe direction and variance closely resemble the direction found by optimising directly for intervention outcomes. A practical, checkable rule for anyone using probes to claim "the model represents X". Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Interpretability as a Science. Authors: Karl Vilhelmsson Emneby, Julian Yocum, Cameron Allen (affiliations not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 16 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in the abstract · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20371

- Agentic on-policy distillation reshapes agent behaviour, sometimes destructively — Distilling an agent on one domain changes how it reasons, calls tools and gathers observations in others. General-reasoning supervision cut mathematical tool attempts from 70.4% to 14.4% while raising accuracy from 16.7% to 38.0%; but ALFWorld supervision cut tau-squared-bench accuracy from 41.5% to 6.6%, with frequent tool calls that violate user-information dependencies. Keeping the top 95% of token positions ranked by teacher preference change lifted tau-squared accuracy from 11.5% to 29.9%, at a cost on the source domain. Relevant to anyone fine-tuning agents on narrow trajectories. Authors: Deyang Kong, Zhaoyi Li, Sijian Ren, Ying Wei, Defu Lian, Shikun Zhang (affiliations not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 14 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in the abstract · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19244

- Latent feedback beats repeating data when unique data is limited — Feeding each token's top-layer state into the next token's input is compared against a vanilla Transformer that spends the same compute on more epochs, with both tuned at every size. Latent feedback saves up to 48% of compute and 47% of unique data; the savings grow with unique data per parameter and with repetition. One scaling law fitted on 60M to 271M models predicts a held-out 778M model's loss to within 0.3%. It matters because pretraining is approaching a data limit rather than a compute limit. Authors: Benhao Huang, Chaitanya Dwivedi, Xuezhe Ma, Alexander Moreno, Hector Liu (affiliations not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 16 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code (github.com/ifm-ai/LoRM, per paper comment) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20404

- Claude solves two open problems in black-hole physics (lab research post) — Anthropic reports that Claude Fable 5.2 completed a Hawking-Page transition calculation set up more than 20 years ago, showing that a sudden transition associated with black-hole formation persists where spacetime no longer has a definite shape, and found the exact limit of the simplest version of the modular bootstrap used to test candidate theories of quantum gravity. Two preprints accompany the post and the post has a section on Claude's methods. The claim is the lab's own and the verification rests on the preprints; the physics community's response is the thing to watch. Authors: Anthropic Science team · First submitted: published 17 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: two preprints linked from the post · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-black-hole-physics

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19800
