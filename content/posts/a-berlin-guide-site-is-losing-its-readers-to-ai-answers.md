On Sunday 15 November, All About Berlin had fewer than 1,000 unique visitors. Nicolas Bouliane, who has written the site's guides since 2017 and has lived from it since 2020, says it was the first time he could remember a day that low. Three days later a post he had written in May reached the front page of Hacker News, and he sat down to write what he calls a quick update instead of answering hundreds of comments one by one.

That update, published on 18 November, is the freshest evidence in a story that has run all year. The May post claimed that the site, after seven years of growth, had lost 70 to 75% of its traffic to Google's AI Overviews. The new one says the traffic is "still in free fall", with a double-digit percentage lost every month. These are the owner's own analytics, not an audited series, and the only figures that exist for the site are his. But he is a first-hand witness with a stated mechanism, and the mechanism has been measured elsewhere.

## What the guide is for

All About Berlin is a set of long, practical guides for people who have moved to Germany: how to register an address, how to open a bank account without the right combination of papers, how to deal with the immigration office. Bouliane writes that local and federal offices refer people to his guides and that the Berlin immigration office uses his data about itself. The Hacker News thread is full of readers saying the same thing from the other side. One moved from Dublin in 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, and found the site through a relocation agency that recommended it.

The site earns its money from affiliate commissions, and in the update Bouliane says he has negotiated higher commissions and moved from aggregation to curation, telling readers what he uses rather than listing a hundred options. He says he now earns more per visitor than a few years ago, which partly makes up for the shrinking audience. His income is "much lower than last year", but it has somewhat stabilised and still covers his bills.

## The change in the results page

In May he described the change plainly. Searchers used to get a link to his site. Now they get a page of ads, then an AI-generated answer "trained on my content", then, last, a link to his site. In the same post he wrote that Google had made two announcements the week before: that AI-generated answers will replace search results entirely, and that those answers carry ads. The first is his reading of Google's news that month, which he supports with a link to a TechCrunch report. The second Google itself documents. On 20 May it published *A new generation of ads for the AI era of Search*, which describes two ad formats built with Gemini that it is testing in AI Mode, each with what it calls an independent AI explainer, labelled "Sponsored".

Google presents the shift as a gain for searchers. Its post says that 75% of people report making faster, more confident decisions using AI Mode, a survey result that the company footnotes and that this article has not checked.

## What readers do when an answer is on the page

The clearest public measurement of what happens next comes from Pew Research Center. It followed the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025, covering 68,879 Google searches, of which 12,593 produced an AI summary. On visits to pages with a summary, people clicked a regular result in 8% of cases, against 15% on pages without one. They clicked a link inside the summary itself in 1% of visits. On 26% of pages with a summary they ended the browsing session, against 16% of pages without.

Pew counted clicks, not publishers' revenue, and its data predate the AI Mode that Google now pushes. It cannot say how much of any one site's fall comes from summaries and how much from changed ranking. A Hacker News commenter made that second point: Google, he wrote, had been pushing real results below the fold long before AI. Another commenter ran a search for the best German bank account for foreigners, and said that on Google the site came second, under Reddit, among a page of what he called affiliate slop. Nothing in the thread settles which of these forces matters most.

## A dispute that nobody has settled

The thread also contains the argument that Bouliane's situation invites. One reader asked whether a static guide beats an AI bot built on that guide, and answered that once hallucination is set aside the bot plainly wins, which is bad for the writer. Another replied that a summary loses what the reader did not know to ask: the advice that begins "I know you're asking about X, but since you're on that journey, you really ought to also do Y and Z". A third wanted Google to credit the source and share the advertising money.

Bouliane's own account sits between the two. In the update he writes that "Google, OpenAI and Anthropic will keep leeching off of my work and sell it as a subscription, but it will still help people", and that he can live with that as long as he can feed himself. He has set out a list of options: give up and take a job as a software developer, start something else, or double down.

## The plan he chose

He is doing a bit of the second and the third. He already sells health insurance to immigrants through a second business, A Good Start, which he calls "soul-crushingly boring" but "honest work". He plans to concentrate on what an AI answer cannot reproduce, information nobody has yet written down, such as the immigration office's wait times and rent prices, which he has published before. And in 2027 he wants to start a forum, on the view that Reddit killed forums long ago and that AI is now killing Reddit. He says people need advice from other humans more than before, and he is not sure he can get such a community going.

He told Hacker News that next year might be his last running the site full time. What he will not do, he writes, is let All About Berlin "enshittify", and he ends the update with four lines of Dylan Thomas: rage, rage against the dying of the light.
