## Papers worth a deep dive

- Interpreting Language Model Parameters — Most interpretability work studies activations; this paper decomposes the weights themselves. The method, adVersarial Parameter Decomposition (VPD), splits a language model's parameters into subcomponents that each implement a small part of its algorithm, and trains them so that behaviour survives when many subcomponents are ablated, including adversarially chosen ablations. This yields a new kind of circuit analysis in which sequences of interactions between parameter subcomponents produce the output. It is a 124-page paper with an online interactive version, and it targets the gap the authors name: how parameters and nonlinearities compute on representations. Authors: Goodfire (Bushnaq, Braun, Sharkey and colleagues; affiliation inferred from the linked Goodfire page) · First submitted: 14 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: interactive write-up (goodfire.com/research/interpreting-lm-parameters); code not stated · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability of what happens inside a model). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20748

- Language Models Share a Readout Bottleneck in Working Memory — LLMs reproduce the human N-back signature (accuracy falls as load rises, and errors cluster on "lure" items). The paper asks whether the cause is imprecise attention or interference at read-out. In 15 pretrained LLMs (70M to 14B parameters, three families) the task-carrying attention heads attend to the target even on missed matches, and linear probes decode near-perfectly whether a match sat at the target or the lure, even when the model's own answer cannot tell them apart. Sharpening attention gains almost nothing, while suppressing the letter identity of non-target items raises sensitivity (d') in nearly all conditions. The limit lies in reading items out under interference, which matches interference accounts of human working memory. Authors: Dongyu Gong (single author; institution not stated) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21176

- Entertain, then Cancel: How Thinking Makes Reasoning Models Read Some and Or Literally — Holding weights fixed and varying only how long a model thinks, extra thinking makes most tested open reasoning models and GPT-5 drop the pragmatic reading of "some" (not all) and "or" (not both) and read them as plain logic, while context-driven inferences such as indirect answers get better. Cutting the reasoning trace at successive points shows the model first entertains the enriched reading and cancels it later; answering from the early reading recovers the implicatures without losing literal accuracy. The point for evaluation: thinking changes how a model interprets, not just how accurate it is. Authors: Kou, Yang, Hao and colleagues (institution not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes (pragmatics, human-style inference). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21204

- Does Latent Chain-of-Thought Actually Reason? A Single, Unifying Taxonomy for Latent Reasoning — Across 21 model-task configurations (three training recipes, three tasks, two backbones) the authors delete or replace the continuous "thought" states and probe them. On logical tasks COCONUT often already holds a readable answer before the chain begins and deleting the chain barely changes predictions; per-step supervision gives an ordered layout and can create real dependence, yet an ordered chain can still act as a placeholder (deletion hurts, content replacement does not). On arithmetic all recipes depend on chain content. Decodable intermediate answers are not evidence of reasoning; the taxonomy separates what a chain represents from how the model uses it, and reconciles earlier conflicting reports. Authors: Qiu, Yu, Jiang, McAuley and colleagues (UC San Diego group inferred from the senior author; not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21182

- The Thinking Leakage in Reinforcement Learning for LLMs — A careful negative result on a popular recipe. Qwen3 hybrid models are RL-trained in NO-THINK mode and reported to reason better; the authors show much of the gain is "thinking leakage": the update partly reactivates the model's own THINK mode instead of teaching new reasoning. Responses get longer and more reflective, activations shift toward THINK mode, scaling the RL update approaches but never exceeds THINK-mode accuracy, and in Qwen3-8B swapping a low-dimensional THINK-related activation subspace back to the original values removes much of the gain. Useful for anyone reading RL-for-reasoning claims at face value. Authors: Zhu, Liu, An, Aliev, Li (institution not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 9 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes (how reasoning is switched on inside a model). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14270

- The belief-action gap in language model agents — In text grid worlds, the authors estimate an agent's task-relevant beliefs (report-based and representation-based probes) and its action values (inverse RL). State and value information is often represented more reliably than it shows up in behaviour; decisions sharpen at a commitment point, where reported uncertainty falls, but individual belief changes do not consistently explain shifts in preference. Transplanted reasoning prefixes steer forced actions, while the activation edits tested give limited control. Some agent failures are therefore failures to use what the model knows, not missing knowledge. Authors: Okcu, Wang, Xie, Dalton, Nalmpantis and colleagues (institution not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 12 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17444

- Beyond Successful Play: Diagnosing Opponent Modeling in Large Language Models — A hide-and-seek benchmark against algorithmic opponents with explicitly defined recursive beliefs, so ground-truth beliefs and action probabilities are known. It separates adaptive play, action prediction and belief tracking. Prompting structure and behavioural fine-tuning improve play, but diagnostics show weak opponent prediction and belief tracking; in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, predictions improve when the opponent's type and parameters are given and get worse when the model must infer them. Good play is not evidence of theory of mind. Authors: Xie, Zhao, Wilson (Robert C. Wilson is a cognitive scientist; institution not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 12 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes (theory of mind). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17310

- When Reasoning Traces Conflict with Evidence: How Answer Configuration Shapes Evidence Following — Tested on gpt-oss, Gemma4 and Qwen3 with HotpotQA and MuSiQue. When a stale reasoning trace is kept and the model simply continues thinking, it repeats the old conclusion in most conflicts; answering from the same trace in a separate call raises evidence-following by 29.9 to 60.6 points, though removing the trace costs accuracy about equally in both set-ups. Benefiting from a trace and deferring to it are distinct. Practical technique to try: renewed reasoning, unlike a "verify" instruction, follows current evidence, and the last line of the trace carries most of its value. Authors: Li, Shi, Li (institution not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 14 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: not stated · Cognitive side: yes (and a context technique). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20805

- How artificial neural networks build their own learning machinery (PNAS, 9 Oct; MIT News, 13 Oct) — Neuroscience-side result. MIT's McGovern Institute researchers argue that biological plausibility of a learning model should include the ability to self-organise: with simple rules about how and when connections change, randomly connected units assemble their own learning machinery and learn pattern recognition and image classification, with performance reported as similar to backpropagation-trained networks. The authors caution this does not show the brain does it, and it is not a replacement for backpropagation at scale. The PNAS page returned 403, so the claims rest on MIT's account and a Reddit thread (r/MachineLearning, 15 Oct) that links both; the PNAS DOI is 10.1073/pnas.2606245123.
