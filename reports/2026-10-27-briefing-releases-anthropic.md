- 🚨 OpenAI posts AI-produced claims that refute the Exponential Time Hypothesis and prove local smoothing and the Kakeya conjecture in all dimensions, and says it has proved existence for Yang–Mills and QCD, this time with outside experts checking the work — This is a material new development on the `openai/math` collection, which this desk reported on 7 October (722 manuscripts from an unnamed internal model). That first release drew complaints from mathematicians about its credit and citations, and three manuscripts were later withdrawn. This round is announced as "three new discoveries in mathematics, computer science, and physics" and handled differently: outside groups were brought in before publication. The computer-science preprint, "The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False" (dated 17 October, revised 25 October, author "OpenAI"), gives a deterministic algorithm that counts the satisfying assignments of any fixed-width CNF formula. It runs in time (n+m)^O_k(1) · 2^(n/(log n)^(1/2+o(1))) and uses polynomial space. Applied to 3-SAT, this would refute ETH, the hardness premise behind hundreds of results in parameterised and fine-grained complexity. The paper itself stresses that the bound is still superpolynomial and does not make general NP problems subexponential. The repository's history says the paper comes with a Lean formalisation. A working group led by Russell Impagliazzo (one of ETH's originators), Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams will examine the proof, and asked for the materials to be made public at once. The second new manuscript proves Sogge's local-smoothing conjecture for the wave equation in every dimension from two up. From that it derives the Kakeya maximal and dimension conjectures and Stein's restriction estimates across the interior of the conjectured range. Until now the Kakeya conjecture had been settled one dimension at a time, with the three-dimensional case proved only recently. The physics result is not public. According to passages from the post quoted on Hacker News, the models produced a natural-language proof of Yang–Mills existence on 9 September and one for QCD on 18 September. The Yang–Mills existence formalisation was finished on 16 October, and the physicists' group chose to write the work up themselves instead of releasing the AI output. Commenters note that this does not cover the mass-gap half of the Millennium Prize problem. The post, again as quoted on HN, also announces about $5 million in funding through a research foundation (commenters name the Simons Foundation). Why it matters: an AI lab's model is claiming to have settled a widely assumed conjecture in theoretical computer science, and expert verification is now set up in advance rather than left to unpaid volunteers. Every claim is unreviewed, the repository warns that unformalised results "could have issues", and 7 October showed that some claims do get withdrawn. The HN threads (570 and 370 points) are split between excitement and objections that peer review is being bypassed. ## 3. China

- Tencent Hunyuan releases Hy-SimulMT, an open model for simultaneous Chinese–English speech-to-speech translation with voice cloning, and compares it with professional interpreters — Hy-SimulMT listens to a continuous audio stream and translates while the speaker is still talking. It outputs translated text and, through a separately configured TTS service, speech in a clone of the speaker's voice. Tencent shows a quality–latency plot for its RealSI long-form benchmark. On it, Hy-SimulMT reaches a streaming lag (StreamLAAL) of about 3 seconds, the lowest of the systems compared. Its GEMBA quality score, according to Tencent, beats the open models compared and four of five commercial APIs. On short-form sets it scores 90.3 on FLEURS, 75.5 on CoVoST2, 81.2 on ACL6060 and 84.7 on RealSI-Sentence. In the same table, Gemini 3.5 LiveTranslate leads on FLEURS (92.4) and ACL6060 (82.6), and Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate on CoVoST2 (82.1); GPT-Realtime-Translate scores 77.7/50.8/76.4/76.7. Tencent also ran a small matched test on 18 new Chinese and English recordings of about 6 minutes each from September 2026. The comparison set was six interpreters with CATTI Level 1 or Level 2 certificates, with the best of each three taken. Tencent says quality was on a par with expert interpreters at lower latency, and adds that the finding holds only for that sample. Interpreting academics quoted on the card also say the system "starts speaking first and makes corrections afterward", and that interpreters remain necessary in high-stakes settings. Why it matters: until now, live interpreting at this quality has come only as a paid API (Gemini, Qwen, OpenAI). This release puts an Apache-2.0 model, an inference server with a web UI and WebSocket API (SimulInfer), and training tools (SimulTrain) in the open, a direct consequence for interpreters and for vendors of conference tools. All benchmark figures are Tencent's own, and there is no technical report. - Baidu's ERNIE team releases Unlimited OCR 2, a 3B open OCR model that parses documents of hundreds of pages in one pass, with near-constant per-step decoding cost — Unlimited OCR 2 builds on Unlimited OCR (June 2026; DeepSeek-OCR's DeepEncoder with a mixture-of-experts decoder). It replaces every decoder attention module with "Sparse Reference Sliding Window Attention" (SR-SWA). A page gate narrows each query to the relevant pages, and a token sieve adds the top-k remote token buckets, so active visual references stay at a fixed budget of about 512 tokens however long the document is. The technical report likens this to a person copying a book: they look at the page in front of them and glance back only when needed. Baidu introduces MarathonDocBench (100 to 300 pages) and reports 77.06 on it. Its chart shows margins of 6.3 points on MPDocBench (up to 30 pages) and 43.3 on MarathonDocBench over Qwen3.8-Max, Qwen-3.8-Flash-Next and Gemini-3.8-Flash, whose accuracy falls as documents grow. At a hundred pages, per-step decoding is 7.0x faster than full attention and 5.6x faster than the earlier R-SWA. Splitting a document into page windows that reuse the boundary KV cache cuts peak KV memory from 47.00 GB to 1.77 GB for a single sequence and raises throughput from 360 to 1,315 tokens/s at batch 16. Single-page accuracy holds: 94.02 overall on OmniDocBench v1.6. The report's stated limitation: the sparse attention keeps no global view of the document, so heading hierarchies and other document-level structure suffer. Why it matters: long reports, filings and books can go through an end-to-end VLM in one pass on one GPU instead of being split and stitched back together. But the recommended serving path is a customised SGLang wheel, and the benchmark that shows the largest gap is Baidu's own. - Ant Group and academic partners release LBS-27B, showing that a "zoom-in" policy learned only on synthetic GUI-grounding data carries over to general visual question answering — LBS ("Learning to Look Beyond Screens", from Wuhan University of Technology, CASIA, Zhejiang University and Ant Group's inclusionAI) fine-tunes Qwen3.8-27B into an agent that asks for crops of an image, refines or revisits them, and only then answers. It can request up to two crops per turn, three per episode. Training used about 10,000 synthetic GUI-grounding examples for cold-start SFT, followed by just 80 GRPO steps (batch 64, 8 rollouts per prompt), with no general-domain or VQA data. The authors report 81.15% on ScreenSpot-Pro, 84.57% on OS-World-G (refined), 89.13% and 87.75% on HR-Bench 4K/8K, and 59.67% on PerceptionBench. The authors note that the comparisons are not controlled: their PerceptionBench judge was a self-hosted DeepSeek-V4-Flash, while reference results used GPT-oss-120B. Why it matters: the result suggests that where to look is a skill that transfers between tasks, and can be taught cheaply on screenshots, a cheap result for teams building computer-use or document agents. The release includes the training recipe and an evaluation harness, and the actions are plain text, so no native tool-calling API is needed. The paper is "to be added with the arXiv release". ## 4. Rest of the world

- Sarvam AI describes its "knowledge fabric" for enterprise agents: on Harvey's LAB legal benchmark, answers met 84.2% of rubric criteria with a pre-built ontology, against 55.1% for agentic RAG, at less than half the cost — The Indian lab's write-up argues that chunked retrieval loses qualifying context and cannot tell an agent when it has found everything. The fabric does that work in advance: documents and data are turned into a knowledge tree and an ontology that agents query, navigate and cite. On LAB, with GLM 5.3 as the agent model, Sarvam reports per-task averages of 0.86M tokens against 1.80M, 8.6 minutes against 30, 14.2 tool calls against 36.5, and $0.28 against $0.64. It solved 109 tasks against 72, with no question left unanswered. The comparison Sarvam draws is with Harvey's own harness, reported at 79.1% with GPT-5.6 Sol and 71.7% with Opus 4.8; the setups differ. On Sierra's tau-knowledge banking benchmark, the fabric matched baseline accuracy with 64% fewer tokens. Next on the roadmap is shared memory across agents. Why it matters: it is a measured case that how context is organised beforehand matters more than the model, with the cost figures shown, for anyone building retrieval for agents. All figures are Sarvam's own, and the fabric is so far a product used with early customers, not a public release. ## 5. Tools, APIs and platform updates

- Gemini API: new paid-tier projects now store Generate Content API requests by default, matching the Interactions API — Since 26 October, `generateContent`, `streamGenerateContent` and `batchGenerateContent` calls from projects created on or after that date are logged with `store=true` unless the developer opts out. Opt-out works per request (`store=false`) or for the project in AI Studio's Logs and Datasets settings. Existing projects keep their current setting. Logs expire after 55 days by default (configurable to 7, 14, 28 or 55); logs saved into a dataset do not expire. Google's logging policy says logs from these billing-enabled projects are not used for training by default. If a developer shares a dataset with Google, it is processed under the "Unpaid Services" terms, which allow model training and human review. Why it matters: teams that start new Gemini projects for regulated or confidential work now have to switch logging off actively, and their data-processing documents may need updating.
