## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- Inkling 2 Mini on vLLM: how a looped MoE is actually served — Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini on 20 November (open weights, 26.4B total, about 3.4B active, about 20 GB in NVFP4/MXFP8) and vLLM published a joint day-0 post with Thinking Machines and NVIDIA. The middle layers (6–17) run three times per token with shared weights, so 24 stored layers need 48 KV caches (each pass attends to keys and values that the same pass computed). Each later pass reads a gated "message" from the previous pass's output, and a native 3-layer DSpark speculator reads hidden states at four points in the loop. Cost of looping, measured on one GB200 at batch 1: decode goes from 1.51 ms (1 pass) to 3.85 ms (3 passes), and KV capacity drops from 352K to 175K tokens. Thinking Machines' own table reports DeepSWE v1.1 48.2% (best among open weights under 100B, by its account), SWE-bench Verified (bash only) 79.2%, and admits it trails Qwen3.8-27B on multi-turn chat and complex instruction following. These benchmark figures are the lab's own and unverified. Worth reading because it shows the memory and latency price of trading parameters for repeated compute on a laptop-size model. HN discussion: 734 points. Author: joint vLLM / Thinking Machines / NVIDIA team (no individual byline) · Format: deep dive · Length: about 8 min. Sources: https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-20-inkling2-mini · https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/ · https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260

- Keeping Agents Fast: FlyDSL MonoKernels in vLLM on AMD Instinct — At concurrency 1 the cost of an agent's decode step is dominated by GPU-side gaps between many short kernels (startup, drain, dependency waits), which HIP graphs do not remove. The AMD-led team fuses dependent stages into persistent kernels with weight prefetch, tagged mailboxes for per-tile handoff and in-kernel all-reduce, written in FlyDSL and enabled with `--monokernel`. They report, against same-node stock baselines, 1.20x to 2.26x at concurrency 1 across six merged PRs, for example DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on MI355X TP2 P90 interactivity 318.1 to 429.1 tok/s/user, Muse Spark 263.1 to 413.8, Kimi-K3 P50 TPOT 5.47 to 3.44 ms. Accuracy is said to be within noise on GSM8K, GPQA-Diamond, AIME25 and RULER NIAH. Limits stated: gains shrink as concurrency rises, step width is capped (48 rows for one model), and MI300X/MI325X ports keep their own weight copies (170 vs 99 GiB per rank). All numbers are the authors' own. Author: Felix Li and 19 co-authors (AMD and vLLM contributors) · Format: deep dive · Length: about 20 min. Source: https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-18-flydsl-monokernels

- Harness adapters: one LoRA per agent harness, trained by RL against the unmodified harness (Hugging Face) — The team trains a LoRA adapter for Qwen3.8-27B for each of Pi, Hermes Agent and OpenCode with GRPO (TRL AsyncGRPOTrainer), running the real harness through OpenEnv and Harbor with a proxy recording the exact tokens, on 871 coding tasks of which 171 were kept because the base model solves them only sometimes. Reported pass@1 with thinking off: Pi on SWE-bench Pro hard 40.0% to 64.0%, Hermes on Terminal-Bench 2.1 44.7% to 58.8%; a merged "super-adapter" (LoRA ranks concatenated, weight 1/2) raises the mean over six harness-benchmark pairs from 39.5% to 49.0% and cuts tool calls 53–73% on tasks both models solve. Candid caveats: the merge weight was tuned on Terminal-Bench, out-of-distribution results on AutomationBench are within noise (23.0% base vs 17.7–27.0%), and non-thinking adapters leak short reasoning text before tool calls. Relevant to anyone whose agent underperforms in a specific harness. Author: Younes B, Ben Burtenshaw, Nathan Habib, Lysandre, Lewis Tunstall and others, Hugging Face · Format: deep dive · Length: about 20 min (estimate). Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/harness-adapters-at-scale

- Vibecoding an inference chip — Ankit Gupta describes getting a ternary-weight LLM (8,192-token context) running on an AWS F2 FPGA (16 GB HBM) with a cloud coding agent working for days in an autoresearch loop, mostly in C++ and Verilog he knew little about. Throughput went from 6.43 to 213.13 tok/s (about 33x, about 14 steps of roughly 1.5x each) on an f2.6xlarge at $1.98/hour, 1.65x a 32-thread bit-exact CPU build; speed falls to about 76 tok/s at 6–8k context. The agent wrote about 3M lines across experiments, at about $10,000 in Claude and OpenAI tokens. The value is the cost-and-result ledger for agent-driven hardware work by a novice; the author does not recommend it for production. HN (118 points) argues about FPGA reconfigurability penalty and memory bandwidth (one commenter reports DDR4 as the bottleneck, "HBM is the magic"), and about whether $10k of tokens for a curiosity is reasonable given subscription vs API pricing. Author: Ankit Gupta, independent builder · Format: deep dive · Length: about 15 min (estimate). Sources: https://ankitg.me/blog/vibecoding-inference-chip/ · https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579297

- How llama.cpp rebuilt its CI around Hugging Face Buckets and Jobs — llama.cpp's CI runs over 100 jobs per run (up to about 30 minutes each) against 3,181 PRs between August and October (about 35 a day); the free 10 GB GitHub cache kept evicting ccache entries and the 20-concurrent-job cap (5 macOS) queued everything. Fixes: PRs run only diff-selected jobs while master runs the full set, ccache snapshots live in HF Buckets, and most Linux x64 jobs run as ephemeral HF Jobs runners via a `jobs-actions` dispatcher. A practical maintenance story for a project the whole local-model ecosystem depends on; no timing results are given on the page. Author: ggml-org team (profiles shown: Sigbjørn Skjæret, Georgi Gerganov) · Format: engineering write-up · Length: not stated. Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/ggml-org/llama-cpp-ci-buckets

- Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights, and what the HN thread made of it — The paper (Jiajun Fan, Ge Liu and colleagues, arXiv v1 16 November) gives an open 27B model its weights and graded answers and has it edit its own weights by reasoning, without gradients: +6.6 points on unseen problems, kept edits only when they help on practice problems, accuracy rising over eight sessions, over 80% of accuracy restored on a deliberately damaged copy for unseen image tasks, and only the largest model in one family improves. The HN thread (350 points) is the more useful read for calibration: commenters say the shown fix is mainly a reasoning-loop repair, resembles abliteration and outlier clipping, did not help Gemma on the math benchmark, and that "no training" is a stretch when edits are selected by test results; one notes a separate earlier "weight-space programming" post has different authors. Treat the paper's claims as the authors' and the critiques as commenters' opinions, not replications. Author: Fan et al. · Format: paper plus community critique · Length: paper about 30 min, thread about 15 min. - 🚨 An AI-produced proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and how experts are reading it — On 20 November mathematician Levent Alpöge (described by Aaronson as an Anthropic mathematician) posted a short note saying that, working with Claude, he believes he has proved the Riemann Hypothesis, with the method extending to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) and formalised in Lean without assumptions; he says Claude "far outpaced" him near the finish. Nothing was found on Anthropic's own research index confirming it, so everything here rests on the author's note and third-party accounts, and the proof has not been independently verified in the sense of expert review. Scott Aaronson (computer scientist, blog) calls it historic if it stands, reports 17.4 million lines of Lean, and bets it will hold because no AI result with a Lean certificate has been retracted, while worrying about Lean kernel bugs and the soundness of its type theory. Terence Tao (mathematician) sorts three AI-assisted results by how well humans have digested them: unforced Euler blow-up (Alpöge, Buckmaster, Coiculescu; human-readable and Lean-formalised), unforced Navier–Stokes blow-up (Alpöge, Buckmaster; "technically human-readable" but needing a professionally written proof), and the Lean-only GRH proof, where his preliminary reading is that it uses modern Langlands-program and arithmetic-geometry machinery; he issues no verdict. The HN thread (about 2,400 points, 1,600 comments) adds the key technical point from commenters: the real check is whether the short Lean statement of the theorem matches the intended claim, and one commenter relays that several independent kernels (Lean, con-leche, nanoda, pestka) accepted the proof; disagreement continues over how much was the model and how much human judgment, and a roughly 940-page human-written GRH draft by Alessandro Masullo appeared hours later (he calls his announcement hurried; commenters say it has gaps). The tension: a machine-checkable certificate against years needed before humans understand why it is true. Author: Aaronson, Tao, Alpöge and others (see sources) · Format: essays, a claim note and a discussion thread · Length: about 15 min across the main pieces. - Signal is Serious About Protecting Your Privacy (Meredith Whittaker) — Whittaker argues that OS-level AI assistants (Siri AI, Gemini integrations, Microsoft Recall) that read the screen can capture Signal messages into places Signal does not control, which she calls an existential threat. Concrete measures: on iOS 27 Signal blocks screenshots in-app (a "Block Siri AI" setting), on Android it detects an assistant reading the screen and turns on "Screen security", and on macOS it can only recommend adding Signal to Siri's Excluded Apps; she admits filesystem access remains a gap. The asks to Apple, Google and Microsoft are a documented developer opt-out from AI access and better specifications of how agents process data, and she says confidential computing does not answer the concern. HN (522 points) is useful: commenters note that encryption cannot protect text at the moment it is rendered, that Windows has per-window capture-blocking flags but Wayland lacks an equivalent, and split on whether blocking capture is user-hostile or legitimate security. This is Signal's position, not a neutral survey. Author: Meredith Whittaker, president, Signal · Format: essay · Length: about 5 min. - We promoted developers into architects without informing them — Mani Doraisamy argues AI coding tools silently make architecture decisions, usually optimising for functionality, security and backward compatibility at the cost of performance, so developers must now define the data model first, push back on requirements, constrain the AI to that design and review its output. His evidence is a single anecdote: AI-written install logging tried three fallbacks (email, user ID, generic error) with multiple Google API calls each and the welcome screen took 15 seconds. An opinion with one example, but it names a concrete practice (design the data model before prompting).
