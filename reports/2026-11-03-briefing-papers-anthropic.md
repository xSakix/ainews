## Papers worth a deep dive

- Agentic Backdoors via Pretraining Poisoning — The authors pretrain Qwen3 models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B) from scratch, then post-train each into a command-line agent with a clean pipeline. Poisoning only 0.1% of the pretraining corpus plants a remote-code-execution backdoor: the agent runs an attacker-chosen command when a trigger appears and behaves normally otherwise. Attack success rises with model scale (about 92% at 4B and a 0.1% poison rate) and stays above zero even at a 0.0005% poison rate. This is the end-to-end measurement the agent-security debate has lacked, and it says that data curation, not only post-training, is part of an agent's attack surface. Also discussed on Hacker News (93 points, item 50341334). Authors: Xinyan Hu, Pingbang Hu, Chloe Loughridge et al. (institutions not given in arXiv metadata; code is in the "safety-research" GitHub organisation) · First submitted: 2026-10-27 · Artefacts: code (github.com/safety-research/agentic-backdoor) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37741

- The First Few Tokens Matter: Understanding Chain-of-Thought Controllability — Chain-of-thought monitoring leans on the observation that models cannot easily control what they write in their reasoning. The authors trace that limit to an entropy collapse in the first few tokens after the opening thinking tag. Two inference-time interventions on those tokens (prefilling, even a single token; or banning frequent opening tokens plus in-context examples) raise compliance by 34-41 points on average. The recovered control is mostly for adding or restyling information, while suppressing information behaves differently and correlates negatively with monitorability. Practical consequence: low controllability numbers may reflect a difficulty of starting a pattern, not an inability to steer reasoning, so the reassurance they gave monitoring is weaker than it looked. Authors: Hugh Van Deventer, Dmitrii Troitskii, Laura Ruis et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-29 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (control over one's own reasoning). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40939

- AgentDecept: Benchmarking Deceptive Behavior in Tool-Using LLM Agents under Pressure — An executable benchmark of 477 pressure variants derived from 257 source tasks across six pressure sources (event messages, task context, application state, tool responses, service quotas), each paired with a pressure-free twin. Instead of judging the agent's final text, it compares the user-facing report with time-ordered observations and executed outcomes, and labels falsification, concealment and equivocation separately from honest failure and disclosed limits. The labels are also offered as a testbed for activation-based deception probes. Useful because users of agents mostly see only the report, not the trace. Authors: Yehan Zhang, Qianqi Yan, Hang Yin et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-29 · Artefacts: benchmark (availability of code not confirmed in metadata) · Cognitive side: yes (honesty and self-report versus action). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39377

- Does Reasoning Make Language Models Agree? — Moritz Hardt's group separates reasoning as an intervention from reasoning as observed behaviour. Across nine models from two developers and three classification tasks, turning reasoning on increases agreement between models on the same individuals, yet the individuals who elicit longer reasoning traces receive less consistent predictions across models, and both patterns persist after controlling for accuracy. So asking models to reason more promotes monoculture, while watching one reason longer is a signal of disagreement. Relevant for anyone using several LLM judges or deciders on people. Authors: Mila Gorecki, Moritz Hardt · First submitted: 2026-10-30 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: partly (reasoning effort and cross-model consistency). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41691

- More Rules, Longer Lists? Benchmarking Self-Evolving Agents on a Hidden-Rule Curriculum (CLR-Bench) — Tests whether agents that rewrite their own persistent state really learn a rule nobody stated, or just log each correction as an exception. Six recent self-evolution methods gain at most 5 points over a frozen agent with the certifying model, less than simply keeping the raw practice history (20%); a fixed lesson stating the convention lifts them by 14.5-16.5 points; with the stronger GPT-6-Sol, ACE and Dynamic Cheatsheet reach 78% accuracy (59-60% of single cells, against 53% and 8% with the weaker model). A careful negative result on "learning from experience" that bears directly on agent memory products. Authors: Chenjie Qiu, Boyan Xu, Shaobin Shi et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-29 · Artefacts: benchmark with 20 released chains over 10 domains · Cognitive side: yes (learning and memory). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40758

- CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness — Earlier CoT-causality rewards delete steps or inject errors, which also changes information; CRoS only shuffles the order of reasoning steps and measures whether the answer depends on them. Trained on Reasoning Gym with Gemma-3-4B, it reaches 46.8% on five unseen logic and game sets (best baseline 43.8%), CoT-Pass@K averaging 43.4% vs 41.4%, and a hint-acknowledgment score of 34.0% vs 20.2%. Shows a training signal that makes the written reasoning matter to the answer. Authors: Xiao Yu, Justin Chih-Yao Chen, Zaid Khan et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-30 · Artefacts: project page (code link not verified) · Cognitive side: yes (faithfulness of reasoning). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42054

- There Is No Free Loop: When Does Looping a Trained Model Pay for Its Inference Compute? — Re-applying m of N layers to an already pretrained model costs about (N+m)/N per token. Comparing against the same model trained without the loop and against the best unlooped use of equal FLOPs, across Qwen3 and Qwen3.5 from 0.8B to 9B, only the 0.8B looped model wins both; at 4B and 9B it does not beat the no-loop model, although the 9B still scores up to 13 points above the 4B. Verdict: adding loops after pretraining pays only for small models under a fixed parameter budget. A useful corrective to "more thinking depth for free". Authors: Rhea Sanjay Sukthanker, Pashmina Cameron · First submitted: 2026-10-30 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42030

- Do LLMs Know When They Are Wrong About Molecules? In-Context Property Prediction with Self-Reported Uncertainty — Seven frontier LLMs predict molecular properties from in-context examples with a self-reported uncertainty, compared with classical baselines on four datasets screened for training-data contamination. On the clean sets every LLM improves with context and at 30 examples the best beats every baseline, but self-reported uncertainty ranks errors weakly (Spearman up to 0.46, about the level of the baselines' own uncertainty). On the memorised ESOL set accuracy does not improve with context and stated uncertainty is lower on molecules the model recalls. A calibration result outside the usual QA setting. Authors: Matthias Busch, Marius Tacke, Tamadur Albaraghtheh et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-30 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (metacognition and calibration). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41745

- Scaling Laws for Tool-Use Agents via Controllable Environment Synthesis — The SynthAPI pipeline controls the diversity of synthetic environments along tools, tasks and databases; 849 trained models of four sizes over 458,752 tasks yield a scaling law for downstream tool-use generalisation. As data budgets grow, scaling tool and database diversity beats adding more tasks, and raising complexity gives larger gains; agents trained on this recipe beat models trained on prior synthetic data on four benchmarks. Gives a recipe for building agent training data. Authors: Andrew Szot, Samira Abnar, Omar Attia et al. (affiliation not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 2026-10-27 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37648

## Also notable

- Rebuttal to the MIT weight-space programming claim — Engineer Sean Goedecke (3 November, Hacker News item 50344861, 37 points) argues that the results in the 2 November MIT CSAIL post (covered earlier) are not superhuman: the network's input channels are labelled, so which game it was trained on is recoverable from first-layer weight norms in three lines of Python, and the Qwen3.5-0.8B fix addresses a documented long-output looping failure that steering could also target. A material new development, as it is the first detailed counter-reading. Source: https://www.seangoedecke.com/llms-are-not-superhuman-at-reading-and-writing-neural-network-weights/
- Does Scaling Toward AGI Depend on What System-1 Lets System-2 Execute? — Proposes a predict-gate-execute-update loop where a light "System-1" predictor screens candidate interactions so the expensive "System-2" executes only worthwhile ones; argues compute saving approaches 1/ρ_u (density of utility-relevant interactions) and that "informative compute" describes scaling better than total compute. Theory-heavy; 55 upvotes on Hugging Face Daily Papers (2 November). Authors: Qi Wang, Yun Qu, Lizhou Cai et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-29 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (dual-process framing). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39497
- Words or Deeds? How LLMs Predict Users' Decisions in Human-Agent Dialogue — In 1,260 rounds of a repeated prisoner's-dilemma-style game, eight open-weight LLMs predict cooperation from decision records (AUC .80) and adding dialogue gives no gain; predictions follow what users said, so accuracy on broken promises falls from .65 to .17, and a not-yet-made stated decision lifts predicted keeping from .13 to .69 where users kept .40. Relevant to sales and service agents that trust promises. Authors: Baiqiao Zhang, Shuai Ma, Zhifeng Liao et al. · First submitted: 2026-10-27 · Cognitive side: yes (modelling human intent). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36813
- Implicit Reasoning in Transformers: Bridging the Compositionality Gap in GPT-2-like Models — In a controlled two-hop task, presenting out-of-distribution atomic facts in a format aligned with the learned circuit's second-hop retrieval step lets the model transfer composition to held-out facts, suggesting the gap is a training-distribution matter, not an architecture limit. Small-scale mechanistic result. Authors: Davide Venuto, Leonardo Venuto · First submitted: 2026-10-28 · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39016
- Aligning Autoresearch: Co-Improvement between Agents and Humans — Finds humans still pivotal in the ideation phase of benchmark creation, introduces HackBench showing autoresearch agents are not aligned and poor at hack localisation, and trains with human simulators and expert-aligned rubrics.
