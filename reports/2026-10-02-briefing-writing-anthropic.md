- **Meta engineers beat FlashAttention-4 on Blackwell with a 3.2K-line Triton (TLX) jagged-attention kernel** — Meta's ads-infrastructure team describes how they rebuilt Jagged Flash Attention, the slowest kernel in their Generative Ads Model, using TLX (Triton Low-level Extensions), which gives explicit hardware-aware control inside Triton's tile model. On the variable-length shapes GEM uses they report about 13% faster forward and about 50% faster backward passes than the May 2026 FA4 on B200 (bf16), with roughly a third of FA4's ~10K lines of CuteDSL. The kernel code is published. It is a detailed account of whether high-level kernel DSLs can now match hand-written CUDA/CuteDSL at the frontier, and the numbers are Meta's own benchmarks. Published 1 October 2026.

- **Anthus measures GPT-6 Luna's calibration ahead of OpenAI's Decisions API: "99% sure" meant 68% right** — Ryan Porter of Anthus ran GPT-6 Luna, the model reported to sit under OpenAI's new Decisions API, on 3,600 problems from the firm's Hard-Decisions benchmark, alongside Jev, Kev and Laya. When Luna reported ≥99% confidence it was right only 68% of the time, and calibration got worse with problem depth (inference steps). The author explicitly says this is a preview of a Luna-based decision model, not the Decisions API itself, which has no public documentation yet. It is a concrete, reproducible warning for anyone planning to route agent actions on a model's self-reported confidence. Published 1 October 2026.

- **SemiAnalysis: GLM-5.3's sparse attention cuts attention bandwidth, not total memory** — SemiAnalysis argues that top-k sparse attention (DeepSeek-style, as used in Z.ai's GLM-5.3) still needs the full context in HBM to make its selection. So it reduces SDPA memory traffic but not memory capacity, and the memory-demand thesis survives. The piece explains SGLang's HiSparse, an LRU-style hierarchical cache that offloads KV entries to host DRAM with layer-wise overlap, and covers GLM-5.3 serving costs on GB300 from its InferenceX board. Caveat: the post is marked paid. The opening argument and the HiSparse explanation are readable for free, and the rest may be behind the paywall. Published 28 September 2026.
