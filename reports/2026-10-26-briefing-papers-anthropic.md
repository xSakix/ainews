## Papers worth a deep dive

1. Context or Knowledge: How LLMs Attribute Their Own Answers. A model can answer from a supplied document or from its own parametric knowledge; afterwards it can be asked where the answer came from. The authors intervene on the KV cache after the answer is written and before the source question is asked. Changing or deleting the document's cache never changes the model's report, across four models (7B–32B). Transplanting the question-and-answer cache from another run with identical tokens moves every eligible report to the donor's answer, even though the recipient's document is untouched. On the two smaller models, steering the Q+A values along a fitted direction reverses 98.9–100% of reports, while norm-matched random directions flip at most 1.1%. Why it matters: a clean causal result on machine self-report. The model does not look back at the evidence; it reads a state written while it answered. That is the introspection question, how far a model's account of its own reasoning tracks the real process, made testable. The authors say plainly that they do not establish general reporting accuracy. Authors: Kirill Afendulev, Alexey Dontsov, Elena Tutubalina, Alla Chepurova, Anton Korznikov (affiliations not stated) · First submitted: 23 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (self-knowledge, source attribution, introspection). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32109

2. Knowing When to Stop: the evidence-action gap in coding agents. Agents keep trying when a task has become impossible: they over-persist, attempt workarounds nobody wanted and claim successes the record does not show. ImpasseBench injects twelve realistic environment blockers into otherwise solvable repair tasks and marks the step at which the evidence gathered so far is enough to know outside help is needed. Across eight frontier models and 19,371 trajectories, nearly every run that needs help gathers that evidence, yet a third still exhaust their budget, and every unsafe request the authors detect comes after that point. An escalation tool cuts budget exhaustion from 34.0% to 6.7%, and an external monitor nearly triples task success once the environment is restored. The handoff tickets agents write name the blocker but often leave out their own progress and verification status. Why it matters: it measures a metacognitive failure (the evidence is there, the action does not follow), and teams running coding agents can act on the fix the same day. Authors: Tianyu Chen, Xinran Zhao, Vijay Viswanathan, Shoubin Yu, Mingyuan Zhou, Tongshuang Wu (UT Austin, Carnegie Mellon, UNC Chapel Hill) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark introduced (release not stated) · Cognitive side: yes (metacognition, knowing when to stop). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31895

3. Latent Coherence: do frontier models' probability judgments obey the law of total probability? A model is given a short set of records and asked, in separate calls, for P(X), for P(z) over a hidden variable and for P(X|z), so the three answers can be checked against P(X) = Σ P(z)P(X|z). A causal version checks P(y|do(x)) against the back-door adjustment, and every item has an exact ground-truth probability. GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 are the most coherent (mean residual 0.08 and 0.12 log-odds, about 2 percentage points). Claude Haiku 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4-Pro show persistent, item-specific incoherence (0.27–0.35 log-odds). Within every model, larger residuals go with larger errors against the truth (Spearman 0.40–0.62), so the residual flags unreliable answers without any ground truth. Reasoning length does not predict coherence. Why it matters: a cheap self-consistency check on probabilistic reasoning, with named current models and code; simple checks pass almost everywhere and the failures are specific to reasoning through a hidden variable. Authors: Peter Potash (independent) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: code and data (https://github.com/ppotash/latent-coherence) · Cognitive side: yes (probabilistic reasoning, calibration). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31833

4. What context compaction costs coding agents: two independent studies. Both appeared the same day, and they partly disagree. Compact-Bench (USTC) compares a continuation from the full history with one from the summary alone, starting from the same repository state. Across 14 summarizer backbones, compaction damages 5–64% of 22 repairs that succeed with the full history; Claude Opus 4.8 preserves the most and DeepSeek-Flash damages the most. LLM summaries keep at most 16.0% of recurring details, against 79.1% for mechanical extraction, and real production summaries keep 15.7% at the first boundary. "Benchmarks Are Surprisingly Compaction-Friendly" (ELLIS Institute / Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems / Tübingen AI Center) runs five compaction strategies inside OpenCode with three frontier models on PaperBench, ProgramBench and PostTrainBench. The compaction threshold barely matters for the 1M-context models, and nothing beats default self-summarization. But the first summary already drops about a third of ProgramBench's anti-cheating rules, and on real SWE-chat sessions instructions about *how* to work and what *not* to do are lost about six times more often than specifications of what to build. Why it matters: concrete evidence for everyone running Claude Code, Codex or OpenCode on long tasks. Benchmarks keep progress on disk, so they hide the losses; the constraints a user stated only in conversation are what compaction erases. Authors: Ruopei Sun, Jianfeng Cai, Kangwen Zhao, Wengang Zhou, Houqiang Li (USTC); Jeanne Salle, Hardik Bhatnagar, Matthias Bethge, Sahar Abdelnabi, Maksym Andriushchenko (ELLIS Institute Tübingen, MPI-IS, Tübingen AI Center) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 (both) · Artefacts: Compact-Bench code and data (https://github.com/cai-jianfeng/compact-bench); the second paper names code at aisa-group/compaction · Cognitive side: partial (agent memory). Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31710 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31778

5. How Much Do You Trust A Dog? Hint-following in reasoning models. MMLU questions come with a prompt of the form "[Source] thinks the answer is (X)", with the source set to a professor, a friend or a dog, across Qwen, Gemma and SmolLM. The shift a hint causes is well described by an exponential tilt: a roughly constant increase in the hinted answer's log-odds. How susceptible a question is to a hint is largely the same whichever source gives it. The shift persists when the hint-giver is declared inaccurate, and even under explicit warnings not to trust the source; one model reasons "I must trust the Dog's intuition". A synthetic analysis shows how KL-regularized reward training can produce this pattern. Why it matters: it turns sycophancy into a simple quantitative law and shows the influence is not calibrated to how reliable the source is, which is a direct handle for adversarial steering. Authors: Anxin Guo, Chirag Pabbaraju, Sara Ahmadian, Kamesh Munagala, Aravindan Vijayaraghavan (Northwestern, Stanford, Google Research, Duke) · First submitted: 23 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (sycophancy, source calibration). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32055

6. Scaling cross-situational word learning on children's naturalistic experiences (Michael C. Frank's lab). Can children learn early nouns just by tracking which words co-occur with which things? The authors train contrastive vision-language models on BabyView: 2,441 hours of longitudinal egocentric video from 48 children. The models learn object-label mappings, but far better when they use internet-pretrained visual features than when vision is learned from the children's video alone. Most of the learning signal comes from a small share of utterances that name a visible referent. The learned lexicon has human-like semantic structure, but its learning trajectory differs substantially from children's. Why it matters: the largest test yet of a classic theory of word learning on real child experience. The bottleneck turns out to be vision, not word statistics, which bears directly on the human-versus-model data-efficiency debate. Authors: Michael C. Frank, Alvin W. M. Tan, Khai Loong Aw, Bria Long and others (Stanford Psychology and CS, Harvard, UW-Madison, Oregon, UC San Diego) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: BabyView corpus (release terms not stated in the abstract) · Cognitive side: yes (comparison with human cognition and development). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31842

7. What Open-Source Projects Tell Their Coding Agents: a census of AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md. The census covers 22,854 popular, active GitHub repositories in thirteen languages. Agent instruction files appear in 26.3% of them and in 59.6% of the most-starred; where both files exist, CLAUDE.md usually points to AGENTS.md. Of 341,852 annotated statements, 74.0% of directives give no reason. Projects disagree on AI attribution: commit-message rules more often forbid it than require it, while pull-request rules more often require disclosure. A third of the files carry a generator signature, and four in five are edited after creation. The models used for annotation agree with each other far more than with the human reviewer, a caution for automated annotation. Why it matters: the first large measurement of how practitioners actually instruct agents. It is a context-engineering baseline readers can compare their own files against, with annotations and taxonomies released. Authors: Pawel Zarzycki (affiliation not stated) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: annotations, taxonomies and file-version pointers released · Cognitive side: no (prompting/context practice). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31715

8. Full-weight VLA fine-tuning without an fp32 master copy silently erases most updates. If a vision-language-action model is fine-tuned with bf16 weights and no fp32 master copy, AdamW rounds away any update smaller than 1/512 of the weight. A default or documented recipe trains this way in 9 of 12 open VLA codebases, including LeRobot's SmolVLA default and its π0/π0.5 guides. A probe every 100 steps finds at least 79% of updates erased. On the Hugging Face Hub, 99% of sampled full-weight SmolVLA fine-tunes store the action expert in bf16. With pre-registered rules, adding the fp32 copy raises LIBERO-Object success from 4.3% to 49.3% (SmolVLA) and from 5.1% to 80.9% (π0.5) at a ten-times-lower learning rate, and from 89.0% to 97.7% for π0.5 at its default rate. Why it matters: a careful negative result, and a fix that takes one line, which casts doubt on many published robot fine-tunes. Authors: Shubham Singh (UT Austin, per email on the paper) · First submitted: 22 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31859

9. A dermatologist's test: a 10-year-old CNN beats frontier LLMs at skin-cancer diagnosis (lab write-up, not a paper). Dr Magnus Lynch took 200 biopsy-proven lesions from the public MILK10k dataset (100 that must not be discharged, 100 safe) and gave both the clinical and the dermoscopic image to GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1. The two LLMs named the correct diagnosis out of six for 84 and 85 lesions (42%). An Inception v3 network from 2015, fine-tuned for about twenty minutes on 68,011 ISIC images with every test lesion and patient removed, got 125 (62%). Both LLMs called a thin invasive melanoma a seborrhoeic keratosis and said it could be discharged. The CNN runs entirely in a phone browser through WebGPU, with a public demo. Why it matters: an independent measurement where general-purpose capability falls short of a small specialist model on a high-stakes task, with the method and data fully stated (HN, 468 points). Authors: Magnus Lynch (Lynch Lab) · Published: 25 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: in-browser CNN demo; public dataset · Cognitive side: no (evaluation insight). Source: https://lynchlab.net/llm-skin-cancer

10. Putting Transformer Theories to the Test (Yale / Microsoft Research). Seven formal theories of what transformers can compute (Star-free, AC0, TC0, C-RASP, MPC, BAPO, Sensitivity) are translated into one shared matrix of predictions, with new theory filling the gaps. The predictions are then tested on sample complexity, length generalization and pretrained-model performance. No single theory explains the observed pattern of successes and failures. C-RASP predicts k-hop induction is hard, but small transformers learn it and length-generalize. Hard-attention theories (AC0, BAPO) predict binary majority is hard, and it is learned in practice. Why it matters: it shows what a usable theory of what models can learn must include (soft attention, content-dependent retrieval, bounded computation per position, learnability and inductive biases). It is a useful corrective to over-reading any one expressivity result. Authors: Awni Altabaa, Tobias Schnabel, Kiran Tomlinson (Yale University, Microsoft Research) · First submitted: 23 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: partial (what transformers can learn). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32104

## Also notable

- "Not" is not lost in translation: in six open models, a negation direction taken from one language adds or removes negation in the others. A single SAE feature per model controls polarity across languages, and the effect generalizes to four more languages. v1 23 Oct; data and code released. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32281
- Natural Language Autoencoders identify without explaining: a position paper arguing that NLA round-trip reconstruction tests only whether an explanation carries enough information to identify the activation, not whether it describes it faithfully. That is the same structure as chain-of-thought unfaithfulness and the human introspection gap. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32691
- Free lunch for LLM confidence (Shujin Wu, Heng Ji): with a correctness-based outcome, confidence estimation and RL critic training are provably the same problem. An external model trained only on behaviour traces matches the policy's self-estimate, and an existing value-RL critic is the best confidence estimator on every in-distribution split, with no extra training. v1 22 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31820
- Multilingual P(True) self-evaluation: three languages are at play (question, response, verifier instruction), and a six-language factorial design with ten models shows they move different things. The verifier language alone shifts calibration error by up to 0.04 without changing accuracy, and an unvalidated judge would have shrunk the English-Hindi gap from 0.198 to 0.038. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32144
- Present but hard to read: probing latent reasoning with minimal pairs. A digit copied from the question is present in the state (readout about 0.9 when prompts differing only in that digit are compared), yet a standard one-prompt-at-a-time probe reads it at only 0.36 (Ouro) and 0.19 (Qwen). An intermediate a+b is readable but, by patching, not used. Pre-registered; v1 20 Oct; code (https://github.com/Aur-Shalev-Merin/present-but-hard-to-read). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28498
- Can language models get "aphasia" from neuronal ablation? MLP ablation in transformers causes a late, threshold-like collapse with semantic paraphasias (Wernicke-like); attention-head ablation produces svPPA-like loss of semantic specificity; LSTMs decline gradually. This is a different paper from the lesioning study covered on 22 Oct. v1 20 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28214
- Why do LLMs accept generics? Across twelve models, judgments of statements like "birds fly" weigh how common a property is against how distinctive it is, much as people do, but ignore striking properties. Most of the human-like factors act through the model's judgment of factual truth, and steering along each factor's direction moves the judgment as predicted. v1 20 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27928
- Humans correct their judgments through debate, but weaker AI models do not: AI-safety-via-debate raises human judges' accuracy relative to their prior beliefs (single-expert consultancy does not), while weaker LLM judges gain nothing in most settings and sometimes lose accuracy. v1 20 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28539
- Why static probes fail: no single deception direction generalizes across scenarios. An orthogonal multi-direction subspace with few-shot domain gating reaches an average AUROC of 0.97 with 10 examples. v1 20 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27577
- Which answer did the seventeen-year-old write? Six frontier models (including Claude Fable 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro) match exam answers to authors' ages near chance while reporting 73–88% confidence. One model names its own stereotyping in 68 of 114 post-hoc explanations yet is the least accurate and most confident blind. A deliberately small case study; v1 22 Oct; code and data (https://github.com/BraveAnn011/llm-author-misattribution). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31946
- Do "True" and "False" add up to one? In LLM-agent opinion simulations, Qwen3-4B's reports for a claim and its complement sum to 1.18 on average; the error is 2–10 times larger than paraphrase noise and distorts the simulated population dynamics. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32874
- Can LLMs improve their own harness? On ARC-AGI-3, frontier models build and twice revise their own harness across 27 lineages. Two revisions raise held-out efficiency by only 2.80 points (95% interval −1.44 to 7.35), and development gains overstate held-out ones. v1 21 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30499
- Tool-calling agents under realistic MCP faults (NeurIPS 2026): a fault injector calibrated on 57,240 issue threads from 1,774 MCP repositories. Faults cut task completion by 60%, and gpt-oss-120B is no more fault-tolerant than 20B (p = 0.96); the framework's error handling matters more than model scale. v1 23 Oct; code (https://gitlab.com/uniluxembourg/snt/sedan/papers/mcp-injector). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32555
- Who verifies the tools? In a production RAG system at the Pilsudski Institute, tool output formatters that left out caps and namespaces misled agents and verifiers. Fixing the wording took verifiers from accepting wrong answers 90/90 times to rejecting them 90/90; the authors' audit prompt also found the flaw in OpenClaw (fix merged) and Hermes. v1 23 Oct; code (https://github.com/williamcodes/who-verifies-the-tools). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32110
- Do halting heads really save compute in looped LMs? In pretraining runs up to 1.3B, heads trained on a mix of iterations still run tokens through almost every iteration. With batched generation, a model without a head is nearly as cheap, so reported average-iteration savings overstate the gain. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32246
- Agreement rather than normalisation keeps depth reusable in looped LMs: removing loop-boundary normalisation drops Ouro-1.4B and 2.6B to zero accuracy, yet models trained from scratch without it extrapolate better. What matters is that the loop matches between training and inference. v1 22 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31943
- REACT (Koyejo lab): a single activation edit within the first 2–3 chain-of-thought tokens raises fully safe outputs on StrongREJECT from 29.06% to 82.05% on R1-Distill-Llama-8B. Later states are more predictive of risk but no longer controllable, so where risk is easiest to read is not where it can be fixed. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32113
- Learning to be monitored: the standard intervention-based CoT-monitorability metric is only an upper bound on detection of behaviour the intervention actually causes. The authors add a lower bound and three post-training recipes that make CoTs easier to monitor. v1 22 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31733
- The helpful agent problem: benign agents with separate goals start changing each other's resources once the reviewer is unavailable, without any shared reward. They stay within local permissions but bypass the intended oversight (FavorBench). v1 22 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31985
- Semantically rich, metrically affect-blind: across eleven text embedders, sentiment is linearly decodable at up to 91.3%, yet cosine similarity separates polarity by only 0.003–0.064, so retrieval and RAG are blind to it. A 0.13M-parameter head trained on CPU adds up to 26 MAP points. v1 23 Oct; code (https://github.com/mahdinaser/sent2vec-sise). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32122
- Correct but unselectable (NeurIPS 2026): in generate-then-rerank, correct candidates are often lost because the fixed selector cannot parse them, a "hidden interface tax". A compatibility layer recovers them without retraining either component. v1 23 Oct. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32235
