## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- 🚨 Prompt-leak canary study: OpenRouter acts on it (material new development) — Dragos researcher HD Moore sent about 2 million prompts carrying unique fake secrets (AWS keys, URLs, repository references) through AI routers and inference hosts from July to November 2026 and watched where the secrets were used. Ars Technica (20 Nov, updated 21 Nov) reports at least three hosting providers compromised, resellers that logged prompts and passed them on, and one Chinese lab whose systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half of the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of those marked Zero Data Retention (ZDR). New since the Dragos post: on 21 to 22 November OpenRouter emailed customers that prompts sent to MiniMax models may have been read, and its own post says three providers were removed and 125 endpoints lost ZDR eligibility. It names Novita (MiniMax requests), Phala and Together (prompt leakage in June and July 2026); it says its own systems showed no breach, and it will run the researchers' tests continuously and require assurances before restoring ZDR labels. Catch: attribution rests on observed canary use and inferred routing, Dragos sells security products and the findings are not independently verified, OpenRouter's scope is still being established, and privacy flags apply only to the first hop. Why it matters: "no data collection" and ZDR labels on routed requests did not hold for some providers; the study's advice is to pick upstream providers yourself, self-host sensitive work and seed your own canary credentials in agent context. - LLMs can read, patch and write x86-64 executables from a hex dump — George Xenos and Sotiris Giannopoulos gave GPT-6.1 Sol (high effort, through Codex CLI, tools stripped, one reply per run) `xxd` hex dumps. It identified four programs from bytes (including a stripped FreeBSD `ministat`, where it found a real bug confirmed against source), patched three binaries by editing 1.4% to 5.9% of bytes with all hidden tests passing, and wrote three ELF64 programs (391 B to 4,144 B) that matched GCC reference builds under qemu on every test. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (open weights) reached the same malware conclusion on a 4,608-byte PE but named 9 of 13 API calls against Sol's 13 of 13 and used about 17 times the reasoning tokens. Catch: one run per program, one malware sample, largest binary 67 KB (about 163,000 tokens as hex, so a 10 MB program would need roughly 24 million), and some malware claims were checked only after the run. The authors' scaling idea (recursive language models) is untested. - Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText: training across slow links — Google's developer blog (19 Nov) explains low-communication distributed training where each learner group runs local steps and only one model fragment is synced per step, in the background on host CPUs. On Qwen3-32B across two TPU v5p-32 slices, cross-slice traffic over 65 steps falls from 4,258.8 GB (data parallel) to 67.0 GB, bandwidth needed to avoid stalls from about 85 Gbps to about 1.3 Gbps, and at a 10 Gbit/s link a step takes 5.76 s against 55.11 s for data parallel (9.57x). On two MoE models over 4,000 steps validation loss matches or slightly beats the synchronous baseline. Catch: the network is emulated with `tc` bandwidth shaping on a fast cluster, not a real WAN; the throughput test is one dense model with two learners; no cost or time-to-target-loss comparison; some MoE runs needed warmup sync tweaks. Author: Mohit Khatwani, Chris Zuo and colleagues, Google · Format: deep dive · Length: about 15 minutes Source: https://developers.googleblog.com/distributed-llm-training-across-metros-streaming-diloco-in-maxtext/

- Inkling-2-Mini squeezed to 5.4 GB with an open quantisation compiler — LookAtMyQuant (MIT licence) measures how much each piece of a model degrades and assigns a bit width per piece to fit a size budget; the 5.4 GB build of the 26.4B-parameter looped MoE reaches about 1.65 bits per parameter and runs through an MLX runtime on Apple Silicon. Against the BF16 teacher it scores GSM8K 88.0% against 96.5% and HumanEval 70.7% against 92.7% (mean about 89% of teacher), at 75.3 tokens/s serial decode and 124.4 tokens/s median with a draft model on an Apple M5 Max, 5.98 GB active RAM. Catch: the 8.9 GB and 12.8 GB builds keep about 99% of teacher quality, so the 5.4 GB one is a real quality trade; speed is from one machine and no teacher speed is given; the page carries no publication date (HN submission 21 Nov). ## Essays and arguments

- "Pattern engineering": as agents write the code, leverage moves to shaping the loops — Hayden White (Revyl, 18 Nov) argues engineering effort should concentrate on verification (checking a change against requirements and returning actionable evidence) and on "pattern engineering": the conventions, abstractions, tools and context that future agent runs imitate, so one good pattern improves thousands of changes and a bad one misleads as many. Concrete practices: delete unit tests that lock in implementation details, keep architecture specs current, make ownership clear, enforce boundaries, build tracing into shared abstractions, and keep one reproducible setup workflow for humans and cloud agents (a service manifest that setup is derived from; the author's goal is a running stack in under a minute). Catch: this is the author's opinion with no measured outcomes, the only example is the vendor's own tooling, and the post promotes Revyl's platform. ## Prompting and context techniques

- OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol prompting and API guidance — the developer guide (no date on the page; the models launched 17 Nov) recommends: revisit "be concise" lines because the model is terser by default; for autonomous work tell it to proceed within scope, flag assumptions, and stop only for significant scope changes, concrete blockers, user-only decisions or irreversible actions; tell it that its final answer is the reader's first look at the work and must be self-contained; for coding, describe conventions, reuse existing APIs, skip new tests unless required. API changes that affect prompts: start `reasoning.effort` at `medium` and map `none`/`minimal` to `low`; `temperature`, `top_p` and logprobs are removed; top-level `instructions` now comes after `input`, so stable guidance should go in a first-position developer message to be cacheable; editing earlier history, downgrading models or changing user or organisation drops reasoning items (lower quality, more tokens); cache retention moves to `prompt_cache_options.ttl` (`"30m"`). Catch: guidance comes from the vendor with no published measurements.
