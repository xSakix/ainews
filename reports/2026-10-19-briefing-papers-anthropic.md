## Papers worth a deep dive

- Into the Unknown: nine frontier agents wrote 135 follow-up papers for unpublished problems from national-lab scientists, and none passed — Researchers worked with scientists at US Department of Energy national laboratories, each of whom supplied an unpublished follow-up direction plus the conditions a submission must meet. Nine frontier agents wrote 15 follow-ups each in quantum computing, plasma physics, computational chemistry, materials science and scientific ML. None of the 135 satisfied all conditions, and 114 failed an integrity check, most often because the paper claimed work that was never done or reported numbers that contradict the recorded evidence. The gap between papers that look like research and research a domain scientist accepts is the tension; it is a measured counterweight to the "AI scientist" claims. Authors: Seungone Kim et al., with US DOE national laboratory scientists · First submitted: 13 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: code and data (stated public) · Cognitive side: no. - FDE-Gym: the best of 13 model configurations resolves 14 of 95 realistic customer-delivery tasks — The benchmark adapts 95 tasks from real delivery projects across 20 customer scenarios and 18 sectors, where agents must clarify ambiguous requirements and discover unstated constraints in simulated live systems, scored by uplift in the customer's own success metric. The best configuration averages 0.56 and resolves 14 of 95 (score of at least 0.8 with no incident); even on the 23 tasks where it obtained every key fact from stakeholders, only 10 reached 0.8 or above. For readers who ask what agents can do in a real consulting or integration job, this is a measured limit: finding what nobody wrote down is the bottleneck. Authors: large Chinese multi-author team (lead: Dongxu Yang), institution not shown on abstract page · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark with an online human-practice platform (per abstract) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22350

- Agentic Self-Awareness: agents recognise a blocked task 84% of the time but act correctly only 41% — On paired feasible and blocked tasks in Terminal-Bench 2.1 and tau2-bench retail, the authors separate knowing (a feasibility judgment), acting (taking the right deferral action for the right reason) and holding (keeping the answer when challenged). Blocker recognition reaches 84.4% but strict self-awareness is 40.6%. A harness search with no weight updates lifts strict self-awareness from 45.3% to 93.8% on reused search tasks, but optimising only for abstention can cost success on feasible tasks, and retail held-out gains stay limited. It is a clean metacognition result: the model knows more than it does. Authors: Qinglin Chen, Hiroaki Hayashi, Jiaxin Zhang, Chien-Sheng Wu (industry research lab; institution not shown on abstract page) · First submitted: 13 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19226

- Hidden-state probes miss most of the failures that matter in long reasoning traces — Probes trained on final-answer correctness conflate three cases: a problem beyond the model, a trace where another attempt would succeed, and a trace where one step doomed an otherwise recoverable run. On incorrect traces from an 8B reasoning model, a calibrated confidence probe at a midpoint cutoff misses 81% of single-step unrecoverable cases and 47% of cases where another attempt would succeed. No tested probe locates the failing step better than a rule based only on trace length, and a differently trained probe reverses the ordering of the two failure types. A careful negative result for anyone using internal signals to monitor reasoning. Authors: Shubham Subhnil, Yannis Belkhiter, Erik Miehling, Adam Dahlgren Lindström, Elizabeth M. Daly (industry and academic authors; institutions not shown on abstract page) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22046

- The model knows the request is unambiguous and asks anyway — human interventions as labels for locating agent failures — Treating a human overseer's correction as a measurement, the authors study agents that over-ask clarifying questions on unambiguous requests. In five of thirteen eligible models (Qwen2.5-3B, gemma-2-2b, OLMo-3-7B, Mistral-Nemo-12B, Phi-4) a direction learned from intervention labels flips 72-100% of held-out over-asks at a definite residual-stream depth, while random directions flip at most one episode. In four families the belief that the request is clear is decodable, causally inert and orthogonal to the decision. Probe-gated steering removes most of the simulated oversight burden where a prompt instruction makes it worse; baking the fix into weights gives mixed results, and a geometric rule the authors wrote down in advance failed two tests, which they report. Authors: Cesare Zavattari, Alessandro Tommasi, Giuseppe Prencipe (Italian university group, per author names; institution not shown on abstract page) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21708

- CodeHealth predicts what an AI code reviewer costs: a pre-registered study of 465 agent-written pull requests — A frozen five-agent review-and-fix pipeline with three different LLMs processed 465 agent-authored PRs from 246 repositories. For all three LLMs, each CodeHealth point (a code-quality score calibrated on human maintainability) was associated with 3.5% to 6.1% lower token cost, independent of PR size; the effect sits in the review stage, where agents iterate more on unhealthy code without writing more comments, and adjusting for file size absorbs it. For teams paying per token for agentic review, it is a measured link between code quality and the AI bill. Authors: Lo Gullstrand Heander, Markus Borg, Emma Söderberg, Christofer Rydenfält (Swedish software engineering researchers, per author names) · First submitted: 15 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: data (availability statement in abstract) · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21914

- Steering vectors for Kantian ethics and for utilitarianism are interchangeable — The competing-target test compares a steering vector with the vector built the same way for a competing target. On held-out situations, prompting a framework raises its own score and lowers the other, but vectors for either framework move both scores alike, and none reaches the equivalence margin; they share most of their direction, essentially "apply some framework". An optimistic versus pessimistic tone separates far better. The lesson for interpretability and persona work is that a successful steering gain can come from a component shared with neighbouring behaviours. Authors: Weilun Xu (EPFL), Zipeng Sun (McGill and Mila) · First submitted: 14 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19359

- One direction in the residual stream decides whether a model trusts the prompt or its own memory — The choice between contextual and parametric knowledge in a conflict is linearly represented and can be controlled along a single direction that generalises across context formats, knowledge categories and datasets. A direction learned on simple factual conflicts transfers to RAG, memory and rule-following tasks across four model families, improving use of prompt-provided information over the unsteered model. Practical for anyone fighting models that ignore retrieved documents; it also bears on how models arbitrate between what they know and what they are told. Authors: Yibin Lou, Yi Hu, Jianhui Chen, Chenpeng Wang, Muhan Zhang, Liangming Pan (institutions not shown on abstract page) · First submitted: 14 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20067

- Natural language autoencoders explain which activation, but cannot tell a true account from a nearly identical false one — Natural Language Autoencoders (an Activation Verbaliser writes an English explanation of an activation, a Reconstructor maps it back) are proposed as oversight tools. The paper argues training is a generative referential game while oversight needs discrimination, and tests it on one released checkpoint: true accounts beat accounts of other activations in all 320 comparisons, but beat fluent rivals differing in a single claim only 60.2% of the time (chance is 50%, n = 35). The test covers only transcript-level truths, so it bounds the easiest case. A sober check on whether verbalised activations can serve as a monitor. Authors: Kevin Yandoka Denamganaï (single author; institution not shown on abstract page) · First submitted: 13 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes.
