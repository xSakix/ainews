## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- Progress on provable inference for large language models (Anthropic, 2 November) — Anthropic researchers report FlashProve, a zkSNARK system that proves a given model produced a given response, so a user could check that a provider did not quietly swap in a cheaper model. For Llama 4 Maverick (400B mixture of experts, 4-bit weights) one H100 produces proofs at about 110 tokens/second with about 19 MB of proof per 1,024-token request, which the authors put at roughly 70-95x the GPU time of running the model on vLLM, and orders of magnitude faster than prior open-source systems. Claude did much of the optimisation work; because it is hard to audit, the team ships Lean statements of the security guarantees, a C++ verifier and a Lean-verified verifier, all under Apache 2.0. It hides the weights, not the architecture, and the post calls itself intermediate progress. The source is Anthropic's own lab, so the performance claims are the authors' own; the code and Lean proofs are there to inspect. Author: Roy Rinberg, Alex Allain (Anthropic) with EPFL, MIT and other co-authors · Format: deep dive · Length: about 40 min. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference

- StateBench: testing how language models track state changes over long documents (Ai2, 3 November) — a benchmark of four difficulty levels of state tracking (think variables being assigned and reassigned in long inputs), built to find where each architecture breaks. Pretrained transformers and hybrids all did well on the simplest level and lost accuracy as inputs grew; four frontier models (Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol and Luna) fell to 13% or lower on the longest "general finite" problems. Small 275M-parameter models trained from scratch on up to 256 assignments and tested up to 64,000 showed Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalising almost perfectly on three levels while transformers never learned the hardest one; the authors read that as a training gap for recurrent models but an architectural limit for transformers. Worth reading for the cognitive-side question of how models keep track of a changing world; the training-from-scratch comparison is small-scale (275M). Author: Ai2 team (post by Kyle Wiggers, Ai2 communications), tech report, data and code linked · Format: deep dive · Length: about 12 min. Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/statebench

- Could a modern chess engine on a Pentium have defeated Kasparov in 1996? (Nicholas Carlini, 1 November) — Carlini puts a modern Stockfish port (written with LLM help, which he discloses) on a real 200 MHz Pentium Pro with 64 MB of RAM, playable online. He measures 2846 Elo at 1 second per move, 3180 at 30 seconds, and extrapolates about 3300 at classical time controls against Kasparov's just under 2800; in a 100-game match it beat the 1996 Crafty engine running on a modern Ryzen, with 78 wins, 17 draws, 5 losses. The argument: much of the strength gain since 1996 came from better algorithms, not faster hardware, a useful counterweight to compute-only accounts of AI progress. Elo figures at long time controls are extrapolated from short games on one machine, as he states. Reached 413 points on Hacker News on 3 November. Author: Nicholas Carlini, security and ML researcher · Format: deep dive · Length: about 25 min. Source: https://nicholas.carlini.com/writing/2026/algorithmic-improvements-chess.html

- How we're training a Figma-fluent editing model (Figma, 29 October) — Figma's VP of engineering explains how the company post-trained a model for design edits: supervised fine-tuning on synthetic edit requests checked by deterministic validators and visual judges (a Llama 3.3 70B trained on more than 250,000 examples was competitive with top models of its time), then reinforcement learning with design-specific rewards, now on Qwen 3.8 27B. Reported results for the agent as a whole (not only the model): typical tasks about 20% faster, expert designers prefer or tie it against the previous agent 62% of the time, users undo its work 60% less, and the share of work done by Figma's own model more than doubled, with lower cost per message. It is consequence-relevant evidence of a product company replacing part of its frontier-API spend with a small tuned open model; the numbers are Figma's and the comparison chart is internal. Author: Josh Clemm, VP Engineering, Figma · Format: engineering write-up · Length: about 8 min. Source: https://www.figma.com/blog/training-a-figma-fluent-editing-model/

- Palette: teaching a small model to build decks in your design language (IBM Research, 3 November) — a 20B open-weight model fine-tuned on synthetic data in the IBM Design Language, in a designer-plans, coder-draws architecture with a check-and-repair harness that renders each slide, measures overlaps and overflow, and keeps a rewrite only if the checker confirms it improved the slide (harness adds 3.5 points on average over 131 decks). It ranks first on two of three PresentBench domains the authors measured. The instructive finding for practitioners: a prompt rule could not stop the model inventing facts to fill a table, but showing reference decks did, since the teacher copies examples far more than it obeys instructions. Self-reported on one benchmark. Author: Praveen Venkateswaran, Anupama Murthi, Hamid Adebayo (IBM Research) · Format: deep dive · Length: about 12 min. Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-research/palette-deck-building

- CWE-bench v2: more coverage, quality and transparency (Collinear AI, 3 November) — a public set of 100 security-fix tasks across 91 CWE classes where an agent must find and repair a vulnerability in a real repository without breaking tests, scored programmatically, with a held-out set kept private. Of 11 models evaluated, Gemini 4 Argon leads at 67.8% pass@1 (81.0% pass@4) just ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 at 67.3%; even the leader never solves 19% of tasks. On cost, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash reaches 57.0% at $0.04 per rollout versus $1.83 for Opus 5.5 and $3.92 for Gemini, a concrete price-versus-capability line. The benchmark's authors run the leaderboard, and only 11 models are covered. Author: Gonzalo Gonzalez, Ajay Jagannath, Nazneen Rajani (Collinear AI) and Nathan Habib (Hugging Face) · Format: benchmark write-up · Length: about 6 min. Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/cwe-bench

- Transformers Model Integration Contracts (Hugging Face, 3 November) — a post-mortem-style account of a bug that sat in Transformers main for almost two months: enabling PyTorch SDPA for T5 on 30 July let UMT5's decoder attend to future tokens, so it could peek at the answer in training, with no crash. The proposed fix is a shrunk "fixture" model (66 MB against a 1,227 MB checkpoint) plus a runnable usage path and recorded baselines, so a cheap CPU runner can catch such bugs on every pull request; replaying the old commits flagged the bug every time. Useful for anyone maintaining model code; a proposed standard, not yet a measured result across the 750+ model types. Author: Tarek Ziadé, Hugging Face · Format: post-mortem / engineering write-up · Length: about 10 min. Source: https://huggingface.co/blog/transformers-mic

## Essays and arguments

- The 'Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen' post is misleading (Matthew Honnibal, 3 November) — new on the weight-space programming story covered on 3 November: the spaCy author argues the MIT CSAIL post mixes experiments that really read raw weights with no tools (only experiments 1, 2 and 4, on networks of 17K-172K weights) with ones that write Python and run the network up to 50 times (all the real-language-model results, including the headline Qwen 7.9% to 48.1% MATH-500 gain). His table also notes the Gemma 3 270M repeat of the Qwen fix failed and that the "beats PPO" claim holds against PPO-500M only on Breakout, not on Seaquest or Asterix. It is one critic's reading of the authors' own experiments, but he cites the post's rows. Hacker News front page (about 915 points). Author: Matthew Honnibal, creator of spaCy · Format: essay (rebuttal) · Length: about 10 min. Source: https://honnibal.dev/blog/astra-read-write-misleading

- Qwen 3.8 27B on my laptop can interpret model weights, too (Simon Willison, 3 November) — new development: Willison reproduces the no-tools "which game was this network trained on" test locally. A 17 GB Qwen 3.8 27B on a laptop identified Breakout (88% stated confidence, 52 min), Pong (75%, 65 min) and Pac-Man (72%, 46 min and 43,506 reasoning tokens) from raw weights, and failed on Freeway; he concludes a patient human with pencil and paper could do likewise and rejects the wider "age of source code is over" commentary. The same task thus does not need a frontier model, which narrows the original claim; three of four games on one prompt each is a small sample. Notes that Honnibal and Sean Goedecke (covered 3 November) argue the same point. Author: Simon Willison, independent developer and writer · Format: essay with experiment · Length: about 10 min. Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/

## Prompting and context techniques

- Agentic cheminformatics with Google Antigravity (Google DeepMind, 3 November) — a worked example of agent "skills": a suite of Cheminformatics Skills built on RDKit with ChEMBL and PubChem connectors lets the model orchestrate and reason while deterministic chemistry tools do the calculations, a division of labour the authors recommend whenever one wrong atom invalidates a result. In the lung-cancer case study (crizotinib to lorlatinib-style macrocycles) the agent retrieved thousands of compounds, ran matched molecular pair analysis (which it proposed itself) and proposed rigidified macrocycles with synthesis routes in about an hour, against days per iteration. A vendor post with a single illustrative case and no benchmark; read it for the pattern, not the timing claim. Related practical finding from the IBM Palette post above: reference examples in the prompt changed output far more than added rules. Author: Zoi Tsangalidou, Kelly Zhang, Sukhdeep Singh, Michael LeVine (Google DeepMind) · Format: engineering write-up · Length: about 8 min. Source: https://deepmind.google/blog/agentic-cheminformatics-with-google-antigravity/
