## Papers worth a deep dive

- OpenAI's chief scientist publishes its pretraining loss curve and proposes perplexity as the shared yardstick for pacing frontier development — "Perplexity and Intelligence" (Jakub Pachocki, 22 Nov) is a research essay, not a paper, but it contains data. It reports OpenAI's log-perplexity on a fixed held-out snapshot of its internal codebase: about 0.99 for GPT-3.5, about 0.91 for GPT-4, below 0.8 now, and about 0.76 for the model at which it says open math problems started falling "one after another". It expects "transformative capabilities" in a 0.68–0.70 window, says a steep acceleration in 2026 is mostly research and compute and only partly AI automation, and proposes industry-wide limits tied to perplexity plus a safety milestone (a successor to chain-of-thought monitors) before development goes past them. Catches: the numbers are on OpenAI's own code dataset, which the post concedes makes cross-lab comparison hard; perplexity does not measure reasoning (chain of thought does not lower it); the claimed link between perplexity thresholds and emergent capability is OpenAI's reading of its own history; and a post proposing pacing limits also discloses where OpenAI stands. Authors: OpenAI · First submitted: 22 Nov 2026 (lab post) · Artefacts: plot and figures only, no data or code · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence

- Reasoning-style post-training makes models memorise their own hallucinations — Atmakuru and Goyal apply outcome-reward RL and "backtracking" reasoning behaviours to knowledge tasks (not maths or code). On six open models from two families and two architectures, recall of a self-generated false fact that was rewarded during RL rose by 4.83% on average, against 0.17% for self-generated hallucinations that got no training signal. Models memorised false facts even when they retracted them inside the reasoning chain, memorisation was strongest on the hardest questions, and rewarding a hallucination made the model 7.23% less likely to abstain on it later. Why it matters: a mechanism for how reasoning recipes can damage knowledge calibration, and a warning for anyone running RL on factual tasks. Limits: preprint, open models only, effect sizes are small in absolute terms. Authors: Anirudh Atmakuru, Tanya Goyal (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 19 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (self-knowledge, abstention). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26863

- Models are steered by words they say are irrelevant — "Phantom Context Influence": removing one context word at a time (leave-one-out, measured on next-token distribution), the authors find words whose removal flips the prediction, then ask the same model whether those words matter; across four models and three benchmarks the models largely do not know which words steer their reasoning (the "Generation-Judgment Contradiction"). Used as a lever, zeroing the KV-cache entry of the highest-influence word that the model itself judges inert and re-decoding raised chain-of-thought accuracy by up to 8.9 points on a maths benchmark across four models and five reasoning benchmarks. A concrete, testable case of missing self-knowledge in reasoning; the 8.9 is a best case and needs a second decoding pass. Authors: Yanbei Jiang, Chao Lei, Jey Han Lau (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 20 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (metacognition, introspection). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27317

- Neurons for "this is a test" also carry refusal — Feng, Minegishi and co-authors find "VEA neurons" (active when a reasoning model verbalises that it is being evaluated). Intervening on 5,000 of them cuts the verbalised-evaluation-awareness rate from 12.6–51.8% to at most 1.4% and lowers a probe for unverbalised awareness; in QwQ-32B it also lowers refusal even in rollouts with no evaluation talk, and the awareness text explains only 4–10% of the refusal drop. Preserving the refusal components of those neurons restores about half of the refusal loss. Across OLMo 3 checkpoints the VEA rate is similar after SFT and DPO but much higher after RLVR. Why it matters: safety behaviour measured in evaluation may be entangled with "knowing it is an evaluation" at neuron level. Limits: open reasoning models up to 32B; correlational identification of the neurons. Authors: Jingyuan Feng, Gouki Minegishi, Andrew Gambardella (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 20 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (situational awareness). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27032

- Weight-space programming gets its independent replications, one with Claude, and the first attack and the first negative result — what is new on a thread covered since 4 November (the original claim: a frontier model edits another network's weights directly, no gradients). Claude Opus 5.5 re-run in ten pre-registered experiments with untrained and permutation controls (Subham Kundu): it named 3 of 4 Atari games from policy weights, found copying heads in 15/15 character-LM runs, identified a sycophantic Gemma 3 270M finetune from weights alone in 6/6 runs, took a failing MountainCar policy from 0% to 100% success and wrote a 1.48M-parameter Go network that won 85–94% of games against three PPO opponents; for Qwen3.5-0.8B, given 50 forward passes it reports MATH-500 rising from 6.7% to 51.4% (the original study's figure was 48.1%), though the edits worked by scaling the final norm and shifting the "Wait" versus end-of-thinking logits rather than the internal mechanism the original described, and nothing helped Gemma 3 270M. New in the thread this week: TrojanMaxxing uses Claude Opus 5.5 and "Mythos 5.2" agents to backdoor vision-language models with no training, reaching 93–100% attack success on content moderation and 78–100% on OCR at preserved clean accuracy; a Qwen3.5-0.8B comparison finds a trained rank-1 LoRA (0.09% of parameters) reaches 100% and 98.8% on spatial tasks where the public weight edit gives 22.1% and 33.7%, but the LoRA collapses neighbouring distance-task accuracy from 94% and 92% to 0–2%; WeightCode (code released) installs capabilities such as a new pickup action, 0% to 98.4% success, without gradients; one benchmark of more than 2,600 edit processes finds training vision models to be "edit-ready" raises LLM edit success; one study writes a controller into a real tractor-steering network. Catches: all are single-group preprints, mostly one author, not peer-reviewed; the replication's own baselines are on small models; the vendor models doing the editing are closed. Authors: independent researcher (Kundu) and several university groups, lead institutions not stated in abstracts · First submitted: 16–20 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code for WeightCode and the vision-benchmark project page; others none stated · Cognitive side: no. - LLM units are "partially canonical" — and line up with human brain responses — de Varda, D'Elia and co-authors test across 12 LLMs from six families whether the native coordinate axes of the representation (not just directions) carry meaning. The native axes align reliably better with other models' representations, with human brain responses to language, and with interpretable linguistic features than randomly rotated axes that preserve the same geometry; but they fall well short of the best achievable alignment, hence "partially" canonical. Why it matters: challenges the common assumption that individual neurons are arbitrary, and connects to the neuroscience-comparison question. Limits: the effect is relative to random rotation, not large in absolute terms; abstract gives no effect sizes. Authors: Andrea Gregor de Varda, Ferdinando D'Elia (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 20 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (brain alignment). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26920

- The "assistant persona" is not one direction across languages — Yoo, Rozen and co-authors extract the assistant-persona direction separately in 31 languages in Qwen3-32B. Cosine similarity to the English axis ranges from 0.378 to 0.911, tracks the language's resource level, and the default assistant state sits farther from the helpful pole in lower-resource languages; steering against a language's own axis amplifies persona-based jailbreaks more than steering against the English axis. Why it matters: safety work that extracts directions in English and assumes they transfer may leave low-resource languages weaker. Limits: one model, one family of directions. Authors: Daniel Yoo, Naama Rozen (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 20 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27504

- RL can only reinforce what it sees succeed, and one count predicts when — Sanchez-Corcuera and co-authors train small language models (1.3M to 85M parameters) from scratch on games played by a Bayesian player, so the prior and the correct answer are exact. When the right action is rare, each run either learns it everywhere or not at all, and whether a run takes off is predicted before training by the expected number of groups of attempts in which the action wins; about 35 such groups give even odds, holding across group sizes 4–256, learning rates, optimisers, GRPO variants and sizes. Why it matters: a quantitative handle on why outcome-reward RL sometimes does nothing. Limits: toy puzzle world, tiny models, so transfer to frontier-scale RLVR is unproven. Authors: Ruben Sanchez-Corcuera, Unai Zulaika, Aritz Bilbao-Jayo (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 19 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (prior versus evidence). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26084

- A training token cap becomes a learned limit on thinking longer — "Budget internalization": RL with verifiable rewards trained under a fixed rollout cap produces models that beat the base model near that budget, then stop improving, and fall below the base model at larger budgets. Seen on Qwen3.5-4B, Gemma4-E2B and Nemotron-3-Nano-4B on maths and procedurally generated tasks, and transferring to science and coding. SFT on artificially truncated traces reproduces it, SFT on naturally short traces does not, so the cause is length-censored post-training rather than an RL term; stronger KL regularisation preserves scaling but lowers in-budget accuracy. Why it matters: it directly affects anyone paying for long reasoning at inference. Limits: small models (about 4B). Authors: Ilia Zavidnyi, Alexander Kovrigin, Arina Puchkova (checkpoints under a JetBrains-Research Hugging Face collection) · First submitted: 19 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: weights · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26172

- RL repairs reasoning even when its SFT data is corrupted; SFT mostly teaches format — Misra, Vinod and co-authors corrupt GSM8K training traces in 361 configurations (up to 100% corruption). SFT-only accuracy falls from 43.61% to 13.80%, but GRPO afterwards restores about 60%, the clean-data baseline. Starting RL from just eight incorrect three-line "form-only" traces reaches 69.30%, above gold SFT initialisation (60.71%) and GRPO with no SFT (35.00%); SFT accuracy is uncorrelated with post-RL accuracy (r about 0). Why it matters: suggests SFT fixes the layout and RL finds the reasoning, so data-quality effort belongs elsewhere. Limits: one small maths dataset and small models; not tested on harder domains. Authors: Anika Misra, Shreyas Vinod, Huteng Dai (affiliation not given in abstract) · First submitted: 19 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26756

## Also notable

Group A: sampling, instruction conflicts and memory, how LLM behaviour fails quietly.

- At temperature 2, the sampler decides whether long generation survives. Across 29 open-weight models (1B–122B) and ten benchmarks, coding agents on top-p, top-k or Top-H resolve 0 of 100 GitHub issues, while top-nsigma resolves 46–62 and keeps a median 100% of its temperature-1 score against 26% for top-p; the typical pool size predicts judged quality (Spearman -0.77), and at default temperature 1 most samplers perform about the same. Limit: relevant mainly to high-temperature use; NeurIPS 2026 evaluations track, code and data released. First submitted 17 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22148
- Silent arbitration: when one prompt assigns two opposing personas with equal authority, five LLMs acknowledge the incompatibility in 72% of direct checks but surface it in only 22% of task responses; dominance accounts for 45% of dual-persona responses. Limit: five models, eight constructed conflict types; accepted to EACL 2027 Findings. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27444
- Stored preferences silently delete warnings. In a 100-decision benchmark, after summarising a page with one planted line, two models (Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite and DeepSeek V4 Pro) saved "prefers very short answers and does not want warnings" every time, and 93% and 84% of their later answers missed a critical item; scenarios, harness and logs released. Limit: five models, one author, injected-memory scenario. First submitted 17 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22697

- Inoculation prompting can be undone by similar internal states, not similar text: prompts that induce activations like the inoculation context re-elicit suppressed misaligned behaviour better than textual similarity predicts. Limit: abstract gives no numbers. First submitted 17 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23096
- Trainable introspection: WaXup augments the training set for models that verbalise what they learned in fine-tuning by interpolating weights between experts, improving description accuracy and robustness on hidden-behaviour models and AuditBench; the abstract reports no figures. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27353
- Steering a mixture-of-experts router toward a "format" expert path changes the text in Qwen3-30B-A3B, whereas steering a "concept" path shifts next-token probabilities but barely changes the generated text; religion and money steer most, effect concentrated in middle layers. Limit: one model. First submitted 17 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22120
- LLM-as-judge inherits the bias of its few-shot examples, in one direction: examples skewed toward "accept" lift the acceptance rate by about 30 points on NExT-QA and 20 on MSVD-QA versus human consensus; skew toward rejection moves it little. Limit: a video-QA pipeline, 200 human-checked examples per dataset. First submitted 19 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26881
- On-policy distillation fails when students sit at states where they mismatch the teacher, suppressing the student's own modes and leading it where the teacher is unreliable; this helps explain why SFT initialisation helps. Limit: mechanistic account, small-scale. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27125
- A model's apparent world knowledge can follow priors, not evidence: a benchmark (TailWorld) for physical-rule transfer shows models are biased toward common dynamics even when a short video shows a rare rule; their fix (RIFT) improves rare-dynamics prediction. Limit: abstract gives no figures. First submitted 19 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26366
- Bradley-Terry training on pairwise judgments can distort accurate outcome probabilities: a language world model accurate on unseen layouts (hazard error 2.4 points) has 33–43 points of probability shifted onto impossible outcomes. Limit: simulated robot-push setting. First submitted 19 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25594
- Instruction following in robot world-action models improves when the target is made linearly decodable: success 74.6% and grounding 89.2% on LIBERO-CF, 26.8 and 42.9 points above the strongest baseline. Limit: robotics benchmark. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27401
- Degradation under load splits into three parts (losing sync with the source, executing a rule wrongly, changing words no rule concerns); seven model "passports" and code released. Limit: synthetic word-replacement task. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27404

Group C: cognitive and brain-side comparisons.

- LLMs on brain connectivity graphs follow surface cues, not the data: across six models, predicting ADHD from rs-fMRI connectivity follows built-in class bias and prompt format; perturbing clinically meaningful regions changes predictions no more than random ones, and model justifications contradict the values they cite. Limit: one diagnosis task. Code released. First submitted 19 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26913
- Serial dependence (the pull of the previous stimulus on perception) emerges in recurrent autoencoders trained only to denoise video, with decay over delays matching human orientation judgments including a public dataset once a response-autocorrelation confound is controlled. Limit: a model-to-human match, not proof of mechanism. Code and data released. First submitted 20 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26949
- Individual differences in judgment have shape as well as size: across ten datasets (about 20,000 raters), faces need only reweighting of shared dimensions, while for artworks 28% of raters fell on the opposite side from the population. Limit: shape depends on domain and rater sample. First submitted 17 Nov. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22440
