## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- DeepCrossAttention: letting each layer choose which earlier layers to read — Google Research's blog explains a change to the transformer's residual stream. Instead of adding every layer's output with equal weight, each layer gets a small learned module that does depth-wise cross-attention over earlier layers' outputs, chosen per token. Google reports the same quality "up to 3x faster" and up to 30–40% fewer parameters for comparable quality, with a negligible parameter increase. The result comes from the ICML 2025 paper and the vendor's own experiments; this blog post (23 Nov 2026) is a re-presentation, and the post gives no independent replication or large-scale frontier-model result. It is still a clear explainer of an architectural idea that trades a little compute for training efficiency, and it reached the HN front page (152 points). Author: Kyriakos Axiotis (Staff Research Scientist) and Thomas Fu (Principal Engineer), Google Research · Format: deep dive · Length: about 8 min. Source: https://research.google/blog/supercharging-transformer-residual-connections/ Discussion: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50672565

## Essays and arguments

- GPT-6.2 Astra identifies the author of an anonymous 1808 sketchbook — historian Mark Humphries (author of the Generative History newsletter) reports that with pre-release access he asked OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra to find who made an anonymous sketchbook held at Yale's Beinecke Library (84 pages, 34 with drawings, three dated). He says it did so in 22 minutes from public sources, found a little-known archival source, predicted that the source would confirm its hypothesis, and that the source then did. In a second test he says it checked and corrected an 8,002-row transcription of a fur-trade daybook against 353 page images using the ledger's own arithmetic, a task he says earlier models did less thoroughly. Caveats: this is one case from one tester who had early access, not a benchmark; the confirmation by a human expert beyond the author is not shown in what I read; he also notes the model writes in odd, riddle-like prose when evidence is incomplete. The essay's wider claim, an "intelligence explosion", is the author's opinion. HN (342 points, 222 comments) argues over the novelty of the find against ordinary archival search. Author: Mark Humphries, historian and newsletter author · Format: essay with a worked case · Length: about 15 min. Source: https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gpt-62-astra-solves-a-historical Discussion: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50643158

- Michael Nielsen takes his book "Neural Networks and Deep Learning" offline — the author of the 2015 online textbook, long a standard first text, replaced it with a note saying he no longer wishes to help train people to work on frontier AI, because the labs are "knowingly endangering all of humanity". His short argument: the risk is best understood as a case of easy, cheap technologies that are catastrophic and indefensible, with the matchstick as the analogy (a one-cent technology that costs hundreds of billions a year to defend against), and a sudden burst of such technologies from superintelligence would leave no time for defences. This is a personal position and a prediction, not a finding; the note links a longer essay, "Which Future?", which I did not read. The HN thread (404 points, 244 comments) is mostly about the loss of a free teaching resource, archive copies, and whether withdrawing it changes anything.
