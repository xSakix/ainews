**Google DeepMind has released Gemini 4 Neon, a mid-sized model built to carry frontier-level software engineering, scientific research, and cyber defense at a fraction of the time and cost of its flagship sibling. Its arrival signals a quieter pivot in artificial intelligence, away from sheer monumental scale and toward disciplined, everyday execution.**

The kitchen table was already cold by eleven o'clock last Tuesday when I gave up on my electricity bill. Spread beside the lamp were four PDFs from the regional utility, two scanned meter readings, and a spreadsheet that refused to reconcile a sudden autumn surcharge. I fed the tangle to a flagship reasoning model and watched the cursor pulse while the radiator ticked in the dark. Three minutes passed, then four. The answer that finally arrived was thorough, polite, and entirely disproportionate to the moment—like calling a freight locomotive to carry a satchel across town. By the time it finished thinking, I had lost the thread of my own argument and closed the laptop.

## The Architecture of the Middle Ground

That small, familiar friction—the distance between a model smart enough to solve a hard problem and one fast enough to live inside a working day—is the territory Google moved to claim on November 18, 2026. In a dispatch published under the signature of Koray Kavukcuoglu, Senior Vice President at Google DeepMind and Chief AI Architect at Google, the company introduced Gemini 4 Neon. Within the expanding periodic table of the Gemini 4 family, Neon occupies the deliberate center. Gemini 4 Carbon remains the heavy vessel for the longest, hardest projects; Gemini 4 Hydrogen handles brisk, routine execution. Neon is adapted from Carbon to bridge the two, delivering deep multi-step reasoning for complex, high-frequency work while cutting the waiting and the bill.

The economics are plain enough to read without a translator. Google priced Neon at three dollars per million input tokens—dropping to fifteen cents for cached input, a ninety-five percent discount—and fifteen dollars per million output tokens, all inside a unified one-million-token context window capable of digesting text, spreadsheets, PDFs, audio, and video together. At launch, the model rolled out simultaneously across the Gemini API in Google AI Studio and Android Studio, inside Gemini for business, within Google Antigravity for paid Pro and Ultra subscribers, and directly in the Gemini app dropdown.

Numbers from independent evaluators suggest that shaving weight did not require shaving judgment. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, running at its highest reasoning setting, Neon scored 61 to take third place overall, trailing only larger and slower models. On the Vals Index, it placed second at 70.9 percent, landing cleanly on the Pareto frontier of cost and accuracy while claiming the top spot on both the Legal Research Bench and the Tax Agent Bench. Across Surge AI evaluations in finance, accounting, and spreadsheet modeling, it ranked first in most categories, read dense professional graphics at 73.5 percent on Chartography, and matched leading systems at 75.8 percent on Agents' Last Exam v1.1. On Zapier's AutomationBench it finished first at 56.3 percent. On the new Agent Arena Index, drawn from more than four thousand two hundred real-world agent sessions, Neon debuted second globally with a score of 1336 and second in steerability, a measure of how cleanly a model absorbs mid-task corrections from a human operator.

The strangest test of sustained judgment may be Andon Labs' Vending-Bench 2, a year-long simulation in which an autonomous agent manages inventory, negotiates with adversarial suppliers, pays daily fees, and tries to turn five hundred dollars into a profitable vending machine business without losing coherence. Neon finished first overall with a balance of 16,843 dollars. In the quieter rooms of mathematics and technical research, it led Terminal-Bench Science at 70.5 percent, RiemannBench at 84.0 percent, and HLE-Diamond at 71.3 percent.

Early enterprise testers described the shift less in leaderboard decimals than in temperament. Thai Tran, AI Product Lead at Glean, reported that Neon matched or surpassed Carbon on overall workplace quality at three times the speed and sixty percent of the cost. Brandon Valafar at Micro1 saw it top their Medical Index and internal life sciences evaluations. Trenton Chang, Chief Scientist at Quadrillion, offered the sharper distinction. A novice researcher, he noted, runs whatever experiment you hand it; a great researcher keeps asking why. Neon was the first model his team observed pushing past failed runs without supervision, hunting for new signals and redesigning its own modeling strategies rather than surrendering to bad results. Andrew Carr, Chief Scientist and Co-Founder of Cartwheel, called it a thorough partner across machine learning engineering and architecture design, one that absorbs feedback and respects prior art.

## Code, Swarms, and Measured Effort

Software engineering has become the true proving ground for autonomous agents, where a single careless edit can ripple across a hundred files. Google built Neon to endure long, tool-heavy sessions—either working alone or acting as a calm orchestrator directing parallel Hydrogen subagents inside Google Antigravity. In practice, that means cutting down on redundant file reads, reporting progress as it moves, and compacting long context windows without forgetting why it opened a repository in the first place.

On the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index, Neon ranked first overall with a score of 58.6, alongside 65.2 percent on Terminal-Bench 4.1 and 46.2 percent at its default high setting on FrontierCode 2.0. On SWE-sweep, it led on the mini-SWE harness at 12.8 percent, then more than tripled to a state-of-the-art 39.0 percent inside Google Antigravity, illustrating how heavily modern agent performance depends on the scaffolding around the weights.

To show what those percentages look like in motion, Google demonstrated Neon driving agent swarms through tasks bordering on the baroque. Inside Antigravity, Neon wrote a first-person WebGL2 freeride ski simulator from scratch using real Mont Blanc and Chamonix elevation data—more than ten thousand two hundred lines of custom shaders, 120-hertz ski physics, dynamic carved tracks, slab avalanches, and spatial audio without a single pre-made asset. Another swarm pulled archival photographs to reconstruct Alan Turing's 1936 Room X.8 study at King's College, Cambridge in interactive three dimensions, complete with King's Chapel beyond the window and a working Universal Turing Machine ticking on his desk. A third took a single photograph and translated it into a one-hundred-thousand-cell Google Sheet containing no embedded images and no Apps Script, relying on more than half a million live spreadsheet formulas to compute edge detection, map three-dimensional depth, and relight the scene across six interactive modes.

Underneath the spectacle sits a pragmatic dial. Neon exposes four thinking levels—low, standard, default high, and xhigh—allowing engineering teams to match token spend to the gravity of the problem. At its default high setting, Neon cuts cost per task by 32 percent compared with Carbon, and undercuts top competing frontier models by roughly 50 to 80 percent on the Vals Index and Agent Arena. Even pushed to xhigh for the most knotted scientific and engineering problems, it still runs more than 36 percent cheaper per task than rival models operating at their ceiling.

## The Quiet Discipline of Defense

Speed and low cost carry a darker corollary: anything cheaper to run for builders is also tempting for attackers. Google addressed that tension directly with a specialized variant, Gemini 4 Neon for cyber, rolling out over the coming days to vetted security teams through its Fairwind Program.

Across the defensive lifecycle, the model posted state-of-the-art marks while spending roughly half the budget of larger competitors. Generating secure web backends on the hard tier of AutoBaxBench, it reached 76 percent. In proactive penetration testing on WebExploitBench v1.2, it hit 51.26 percent at half the cost of competing large models. Hunting flaws in existing repositories on SEC-bench Pro, it reached 90.5 percent while halving the cost per challenge. Validating whether flaws were genuinely exploitable on ExploitGym, it matched frontier performance using half the tokens of Carbon. And when patching root causes on Collinear's CWE-bench v2, it reached 70.7 percent, a 3.4-point gain over Carbon. Karina Nguyen, Founder of Thoughtful Lab, reported that in their cyber evaluations Neon uncovered subtle vulnerabilities other models missed while consuming a fraction of the tokens.

Equally telling is how Google tried to measure restraint. A fast agent that cuts corners or hallucinates a passing test is worse than a slow one. In internal Google Antigravity testing, Neon recorded the company's lowest fabrication rate to date. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, it matched Carbon with zero percent cheating and took shortcuts on just 2.45 percent of tasks in Google's coding cheating suite, the lowest rate across the Gemini 4 line. Its agentic safety evaluations showed a 0.09 percent misaligned action rate and a 99.2 percent pass rate on agent honesty, while trims in over-refusal meant it declined only 1.98 percent of legitimate tasks on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and zero percent on Terminal-Bench Science. Across effort tiers, under-service fell by 43 to 56 percent and overreach dropped by 15 to 35 percent. Against indirect prompt injection—malicious instructions hidden inside web pages, emails, or code repositories—Neon paired with real-time safeguards at its highest thinking level recorded a 0.0 percent attack success rate on Gray Swan's benchmark.

Yet any honest dispatch must pause where the press release ends. Benchmarks are clean rooms, and the real internet is a junkyard. A zero-percent attack success rate under bounded evaluation tells us that Google's monitors and safeguards held the line on the scenarios Gray Swan wrote; it does not prove that a clever payload buried in an obscure open-source dependency will never slip past an unattended agent at three in the morning. Nor does sixteen thousand dollars in a simulated San Francisco vending business guarantee that an enterprise finance team can stop checking the footnotes. The measure of Neon will not be its launch charts, but its quiet reliability six months from now, when the novelty fades and the logs grow long.

## Returning to the Desk

For ordinary subscribers inside the Gemini app, the promise is far less theatrical than a three-dimensional Cambridge study. Neon pulls context from Gmail and Docs, parses messy receipts and dense financial tables, runs live web checks across vendors, and handles multi-step chores without exhausting usage limits halfway through an afternoon.

This morning, before the street outside had fully woken, I reopened those four utility PDFs on the kitchen table and dropped them into the prompt window with the scanned meter photos. This time there was no long vigil over a blinking cursor. In seconds, the model isolated a duplicated distribution fee from September, cross-referenced the exact kilowatt-hour reading printed sideways on the cellar meter photo, and drafted a short, unemotional letter to the billing department. Nothing about the moment felt futuristic or grand. It felt like turning on a light switch and having the bulb answer immediately. That, perhaps more than any leaderboard crown, is the real news inside Gemini 4 Neon: frontier intelligence has finally stopped asking the room to stop and watch it think.
