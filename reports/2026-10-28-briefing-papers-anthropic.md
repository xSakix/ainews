## Papers worth a deep dive

1. Thinking-in-Bets: writing down your claims before you read the complaint stops agents caving to wrong pushback (Google). Agents often drop correct work when a user challenges it, and telling them to "stand firm" makes them ignore real mistakes. The method is training-free: before reading any feedback, the agent writes a private Claim Checklist of its main claims, the checks behind them and its confidence, then consults it when challenged. Tested on five Gemini models across eight benchmarks, it nearly eliminates false reversals in patch review and cuts them more than fourfold on challenged report drafts. Writing the same record after reading the complaint recovers very little of the gain. Gating the record to uncertain moments lowers token overhead and fixes more real bugs than the baseline. Why it matters: it is a prompt pattern a reader can try today, and the ordering result (record first, criticism second) is the finding. Authors: Nghi D. Q. Bui (Google) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: project page, no repository found in the PDF · Cognitive side: yes (sycophancy, confidence, self-checking). 2. Models read a hidden motive that they cannot report: base-model likelihoods decode "subliminal" signals that prompted classifiers miss. Earlier work showed that a model told to "love owls" can pass that trait on through strings of numbers. Here, scoring those numbers under the pre-trained base model with a suitable persona header (persona-header likelihood evaluation, PHLE) separates owl-prompted from eagle-prompted output. It also separates answers from a model with a secret desire to harm the user, filtered until no misalignment is visible, from a model with no system prompt. Prompted classifiers, including the generating model itself, stay near chance. The signal partly transfers across families: a Qwen base model reads Olmo's output and the reverse. The author reads this as support for the Persona Selection Model, the idea that post-training selects a persona already learned in pretraining, and as a possible audit tool for hidden objectives. Authors: Oz Amram (independent) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: code (https://github.com/OzAmram/psm-mixture-test) · Cognitive side: yes (persona, hidden objectives). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36202

3. Language models keep using an analysis they have already abandoned, as people do after garden-path sentences (Nagoya University). "While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods" forces readers to give up the reading where "the deer" is the object of "hunted", yet people still produce more transitive structures afterwards. Across 24 open models (124M to 70B parameters), the sentence leaves two traces. A residue of the discarded analysis raises later object attachment by about three quarters of the effect of a sentence where the noun phrase really is an object. A separate adaptation toward the resolved structure scales with the disambiguation error. Activation patching separates them: the residue is read from role information in the noun phrase's states, computed before the disambiguation, and replacing those states removes almost all of it in the 32B and 70B models. Overridden facts behave similarly, since a corrected value keeps pulling later predictions in 8 of 9 models. Why it matters: it is a clean psycholinguistic effect traced to specific states, and it bears on stale information lingering in long contexts. Authors: Xiang Ding (Nagoya University) · First submitted: 27 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (priming, human comparison, mechanism). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36797

4. Few-shot concept induction: Brown's group proposes how a model decides which features of the examples matter. Given a few positive examples (football, baseball, volleyball), a model must infer the latent concept ("sport") without negatives to say what to ignore. The hypothesis, Amortized Direction Accentuation, says the model builds a summary of the examples in a feature space already shaped by amortized knowledge about which features are useful for finding commonalities. The tests use Gemma-2-2B, OLMo-2 (1B, 7B) and Llama-3 models. A single context displacement vector noisily encodes the geometry of the concept vectors. Adding the average displacement vector to the residual stream steers the model toward in-concept answers. Accounting for the accentuation explains output behaviour better than generic embeddings in 3 of 5 models, equally well in one and worse in OLMo-2-1B. Why it matters: it offers a mechanism for in-context learning drawn from cognitive science, with honest mixed results. Authors: Michael A. Lepori, Laila Johnston, Aalok Sathe, Zhuonan Yang, Roman Feiman, Ellie Pavlick (Brown University) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (concept learning, in-context learning). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35533

5. How humans learn an unfamiliar world, on tape: a think-aloud dataset on ARC-AGI-3. The authors record 144 trajectories from 64 people across 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, with time-synchronized actions and spoken commentary, plus 250 trajectories from LLM agents with and without harnesses. They annotate when a mechanism is triggered and recognized, why each action was taken, and how errors were recovered. Both groups anticipate mechanisms and use analogies, and both test hypotheses. Qualitative representations are more common in successful human runs than in successful LLM runs (50.0% versus 14.3%). Humans more often explore with no concrete hypothesis. Because harnessed LLMs are saturating the public games, the authors argue for benchmarks that demand accumulated evidence, abstraction and subgoal planning. Why it matters: it is rare direct data on how people build and revise world models, side by side with agents. Authors: Shuzhi Liu, Archana Warrier, Dat Nguyen, Chengqi Zheng, Kevin Ellis, Yewen Pu (Nanyang Technological University, TU Darmstadt, Cornell, Basis Research Institute and Harvard) · First submitted: 25 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: dataset (https://arc3-human-think-aloud.github.io/) · Cognitive side: yes (human cognition versus agents). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34523

6. Did the model use that? Eleven frontier models rarely say when a supplied hint changed their answer (Stanford). Self-attribution means naming the outside information an answer depends on, or saying it depends on none. If a user suggests X and the model would have said X anyway, the model independently corroborates the user. If it says X only because the user did, it adds no evidence. The authors test eleven frontier models by removing the information and checking whether the answer changes. Most rarely attribute influential information, and the rest attribute non-influential information. Training the acknowledgment into the reasoning does not fix this, because it does not reach the final answer. Their method, CREDIT, supervises final-answer attribution with counterfactual labels and no human annotation. It substantially improves attribution in four open models, including two distilled reasoning models, and it separates answers changed by a user's suggestion from answers the model would have given anyway. Why it matters: it is a measurable form of model self-knowledge that matters to anyone using a model's answer as a second opinion. Authors: Zikai Zhou, Jiacheng Sang, Yilin Xu, Yufei Jin, Monica S. Lam (Stanford, Duke, Emory, UCLA) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: code (https://github.com/stanford-oval/CREDIT) · Cognitive side: yes (introspective reporting, faithfulness). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35295

7. A multimodal model keeps one number concept in different coordinates, and unfolds it across digits (Apple, UAB). When Gemma4-12B-it computes 17+35 from text or 91-39 from a rendered image, does it use one internal concept of number? Causal interventions find a small subspace per condition where swapping coordinates swaps the answer. Reusing the text subspace on images recovers only 53.2% of its effect, but a single rotation and rescaling raises recovery to 91.4%, so the conditions share one geometry in different coordinates. Of each subspace, 9 directions are read by the output layer and 13 are not. The 13 hidden ones still carry the number, yet changing them barely alters the digit being written. Because answers come one digit at a time, the model computes the full result early and keeps it in reserve: when two numbers share a first digit, swapping the hidden part redirects the next digit in 77.6% of cases. Why it matters: it is evidence that a model plans its answer ahead of what it writes, and that similarity scores such as RSA or CKA can mislead. Authors: Oriol Juan Sabater (Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona), Alex Ferrando de las Morenas, Pau Rodríguez, Xavier Suau (Apple) · First submitted: 27 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (internal representation, planning). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37422

8. Counting the Bandages: time saved by AI has to be read next to the errors it lets through. This independent paper contrasts a "stopwatch" view of time saved with a "ledger" view that sets it against excess escaped defects, and defines one decision number, the break-even defect cost. It replicates METR's developer study from its public data: the same 246 issues give a slowdown of 19% or 24% depending on the estimand (34% without adjusting for a chance imbalance), and AI helped on somewhere between 3% and 82% of them. In GDPval's own review-every-output workflow, review time alone caps GPT-5's speedup at 3.6x. In a small pre-specified test, an AI reviewer told that the drafter erred ten times less often checked 7 fewer of 9 figures and let a wrong figure through in 22.5% of briefs instead of none. Why it matters: it reframes productivity claims for anyone paying for AI assistance, with an inspectable replication. Caveat: it is a single-author preprint, and the experiment is small. Authors: Solomon Shalom Lijo (independent) · First submitted: 23 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: code and data (https://github.com/solomonshalom/tsb-bench) · Cognitive side: no (economy and work). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35942

9. The hidden costs of forgetting: deleting old agent context saves tokens but may cost money once caching is billed (Simula, CISPA). Software-engineering agents resend their whole history on every turn, so trimming it looks like a free saving. Across 42 trajectory-reduction configurations and 9,400 runs of two agents on SWE-bench Verified, even deleting up to 100% of reasoning text from old turns can preserve success, but only if applied with a delay. Agents compensate by re-fetching deleted content, which adds turns and tokens. Under flat per-token pricing, estimated cost falls in 26 of 42 evaluations. Under a caching-aware price model, it falls in none, because reducing the trajectory breaks the cached prefix billed at the lower rate. Why it matters: it is a direct, bill-level check on a popular context-engineering trick. A separate UCSD paper (listed below) reaches a similar conclusion. Authors: Fernando Vallecillos-Ruiz, Islem Bouzenia, Michael Pradel, Leon Moonen (Simula Research Laboratory, CISPA Helmholtz Center) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35496

10. PROBE: frontier coding agents find different bugs than maintainers fix (LogicStar.ai, ETH Zurich). The benchmark is all 2,457 bugs fixed over 18 months across 20 GitHub repositories, with a method to match agent findings to that partial reference set. Every frontier agent tested reaches recall below 21% and precision below 9%. Stronger models raise recall from 8% to 17% but do not improve precision. Yet LLM and human judges still call 85% of the findings valid. The bugs agents find are similarly distributed across agents but differ substantially from the bugs maintainers actually fix. Why it matters: it quantifies the gap between "the agent found a real bug" and "the project would have fixed it", relevant to anyone buying automated bug discovery. Authors: Marco Paina, Mark Niklas Müller, Niels Mündler-Sasahara, Martin Vechev (LogicStar.ai, ETH Zurich) · First submitted: 26 Oct 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark site and repository named in the paper (probebench.com, LogicStar/probe); not opened · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35987
