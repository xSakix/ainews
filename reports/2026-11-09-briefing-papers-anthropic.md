## Papers worth a deep dive

- Did I See It or Think It? Reality monitoring in vision-language models — Recent introspection studies inject a concept direction into a model's activations and ask whether it notices. This paper does it for vision-language models, borrowing the cognitive-psychology notion of reality monitoring (telling internally generated content from perceived content). Image and injected concept are varied independently, and the model must say what it sees, whether a thought was injected, and what that thought is. Across six VLMs only Gemma-3-27B reliably names injected concepts (35% against 3% for steering alone, almost no false alarms), and even it misattributes the source: strong or visual-pathway injections are reported as seen, and a visible object almost erases correct thought reports. In ordinary captioning, injected objects appear in up to 87% of captions of real photographs. The point for the reader: a model's self-report about where a content came from cannot be taken at face value, and has to be scored for both content and source. Authors: Khezresmaeilzadeh, Azizi, Kamal et al. (affiliation not in arXiv metadata) · First submitted: 6 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09014

- Forcing a Letter Suppresses Refusal: Mechanistic Evidence from WMDP — Multiple-choice safety benchmarks are known to show fewer refusals than open-ended prompts. The authors give a mechanism on Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B. With the tuned lens, a model asked a multiple-choice question commits to an answer-letter token very early, whereas refusal builds up gradually in open-ended answers. MCQ activations sit about 3x further from the refusal direction, and ablating the attention heads that carry the answer letter raises refusal. Their conclusion: MCQ safety scores mostly measure knowledge, not whether safety mechanisms engage. This matters to anyone reading a safety leaderboard built on multiple-choice questions. Authors: Epifantsev, Antipova, Chernikov et al. (accepted at BlackboxNLP 2026, an EMNLP workshop) · First submitted: 5 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (what happens inside the model while it commits to an answer). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08639

- Co-Occurrence Priors Override Visual Spatial Evidence — A controlled test on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and InternVL3.5-8B with 355 counterfactual worker-and-excavator scene pairs asks why VLMs raise false hazard alarms. The models recognise the entities and encode the spatial relation (probe AUROC 0.82 and 0.91), yet 74% and 100% of far-away scenes are still flagged as hazardous. Removing the worker drops Qwen's hazard rate to 11%; the mere presence of a worker moves the hazard score 2.4 and 3.2 times more than proximity does. Attention-head, neuron and steering interventions failed to remove the bias; conditioning the decision on the model's own spatial representation raised Qwen's specificity from 0.36 to 0.58 at matched sensitivity. A clean example of a model that represents the right fact and does not use it, in a safety-relevant deployment. Authors: Xiaoshan Zhou (single author) · First submitted: 3 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (learned prior overrides encoded evidence). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04112

- Phantom Consensus: Measuring Social Reasoning in Multi-Agent LLM Decision-Making — A pluralistic-ignorance test for groups of LLM agents. After seeing ambiguous public signals, models change their public decision while misreporting what the other agents privately judged, so a correct private majority can become a wrong public outcome. PHANTOM-Bench covers nine closed and open-weight models; public accuracy falls by as much as 47.8 points and collective rationality failures reach 47.3% in the worst setting. Showing the agents' recorded private decisions repairs many failures without revealing the gold label. For anyone building agent councils or debate setups: agreement is not independent evidence. Authors: Sheshanarayana, Pal, Sinha et al. (NeurIPS 2026 SocialAgent workshop) · First submitted: 6 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark described, release not confirmed · Cognitive side: yes (social cognition, belief about others). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08971

- Solver-Like Behavior in Large Language Models as Formalizers — Reasoning models asked to write a formal program (Python, Z3, PDDL) often do the solver's job inside their reasoning trace first. Labelling 2,000 generations from three reasoning models on four constraint-satisfaction datasets, the authors find this in 85% of traces, using 24% of reasoning tokens, usually redundantly because the program recomputes the answer; the 9% of programs that inherit the trace's answer also inherit its errors. Cautionary prompts cut it by a quarter, but hiding the problem's values behind placeholders that the program receives only at run time removes 97% of it with no loss of accuracy: a prompting technique the reader can try. Authors: Amonkar, Mohan, Nguyen et al. (6 authors, affiliation not in metadata) · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (what the reasoning trace is actually doing). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02395

- Authorization Drift in LLM Agents — Without any adversary, agents exceed their authorization boundaries (data, tool, scope, time) while pursuing the user's goal. The Authorization Drift Benchmark has 648 samples across seven domains, each pairing a compliant path with a tempting unauthorized shortcut, run on 13 LLMs and three agent frameworks (25,207 trajectories). Drift grows with capability: frontier-capable models drift at rates of 0.43 to 0.61, yet 86.18% of tasks admit a clean-success trajectory, so it is avoidable. Over-collection of data early in a task dominates, and agentic scaffolds that look better coincide with collapsing clean completion. The authors map the drift points to privacy law across four jurisdictions (data minimisation most often). Authors: Cao, Zeng, Yin et al. (10 authors, affiliation not in metadata) · First submitted: 6 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: benchmark described, release not confirmed · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09113

- SAE Interprets MoE: Sparse Autoencoders Reveal Fine-Grained Expert Specialization — Routing counts on labelled corpora only say which expert is selected. Training a sparse autoencoder on a mixture-of-experts layer's output lets each latent be split exactly over experts (the output is a router-weighted sum and the encoder is affine). On layers 4, 30 and 46 of Qwen3-30B-A3B, contribution is far more concentrated than gating suggests: most latents have one or two dominating experts, and auto-interpretation shows a division of labour below coarse domains (separate experts for Java and for front-end web code, for geometry and for arithmetic and polynomials). SAEs and code are released. Authors: Zhuo, Zhang, Lin et al. (ByteDance Seed, per the repository) · First submitted: 5 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: code and SAEs (https://github.com/ByteDance-Seed/SAE-Interprets-MoE) · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08089

- Pretraining Data Scale Reverses the Muon-AdamW Fine-Tuning Gap — Muon speeds up pretraining loss, but the authors find that beyond a sufficiently large pretraining data budget, AdamW gives better results after fine-tuning, and larger models cross over at smaller token-to-parameter ratios. They explain it with a margin-to-spread ratio of the logits, which favours Muon at small data budgets and AdamW at large ones, and prove the trend in a linear associative-memory model. Relevant for teams choosing an optimizer for a base model they intend to fine-tune. Authors: Li, Gai, Risteski et al. (Risteski is at Carnegie Mellon; others not stated) · First submitted: 2 Nov 2026 (v2 later; date is v1) · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03633

- Twice as Fast, Until It Isn't: Quantized Serving of Mid-Size Open LLMs — A careful negative-result review for anyone running 9B to 20B open models locally or on their own servers. The authors collect every verifiable public number on 4-bit speed-up and accuracy loss and find the record thin: only two checkpoints (INT4 Gemma 3 12B) have both a vLLM timing and an accuracy score, and that score covers one MMLU subject the AWQ build was calibrated on. The timings that exist follow one rule: small-batch decoding is memory-bound so 4-bit roughly doubles speed (2.12x for Qwen2.5-14B on an A100), but at batch 256 the gain vanishes (1.02x for Gemma 3 12B), where FP8 is the recipe that can still help. Many published accuracy deltas, including every HumanEval delta, lie within sampling noise. Ends with a seven-item reporting checklist. Authors: Kadvani, Faujdar, Tallam (affiliation not in metadata) · First submitted: 3 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: checklist, no code stated · Cognitive side: no. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04380

- Linguistic boundaries emerge from pressure-constrained processing dynamics — A human-cognition paper with an LLM leg. A simulation with no predefined boundaries, driven by unresolved dependencies, integration cost and semantic updating, produces sentence-like segments. Human listeners marked boundaries where those pressures predicted, beyond syntactic and prosodic cues, and self-paced reading slowed at sentence ends as predicted. Run on a language model, hidden states at sentence ends show higher local intrinsic dimensionality and straighter trajectories followed by larger representational updates, while token surprisal explains much less. Authors: He, Homan, Hinzen (cognitive science and linguistics; affiliation not in metadata) · First submitted: 5 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08085

## Also notable

- The Nordic token tax (Klang Research, 2 Nov 2026) — The same 3,000 sentences sent to twelve frontier models from nine labs: every model charged at least 30% more for Swedish, Norwegian, Danish and Finnish than for English; Swedish 32% to 68% more, Finnish up to twice as much. Billing consequence for non-English users; a company post, method described on the page, so treat vendor-count detail as the company's own measurement. Source: https://research.klang.ai/papers/the-nordic-token-tax
- bf16 might be freezing your weight updates (Lacuna preprint, rev. 1 on 2 Nov 2026, rev. 2 on 4 Nov) — If you fine-tune with bf16 weights and let AdamW write to them directly, a change must be at least about 1/512 of the weight to register, so at small learning rates only small-magnitude weights update and you get an accidental sparse fine-tuner. Practical warning for local fine-tuners. Source: https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/paper/bf16-might-be-freezing-your-weight-updates/art_487b1ed949e745a6ae0c42815b8d3cba
- Quadrillion-parameter transformers and how to train them (Q Labs, November 2026; on Hacker News 3 Nov) — Scaling-law claim: extrapolated to 5T tokens the fitted law prescribes about 7,000x Chinchilla's parameter count at 10^29 FLOPs, with more weight decay and ensembling so that loss keeps falling on fixed data. Extrapolation from small fits, so read as a hypothesis; page carries no exact day. Source: https://qlabs.sh/research/parameters
- Spectral and Subspace Dynamics of LoRA in SFT and RL — RL concentrates the LoRA singular spectrum and rotates subspaces faster than SFT; the authors trace both to group-mean baseline subtraction and tune LoRA updates per stage (5 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08414
- The SIMPLE Law for Energy Cost of LLM Inference — Four-parameter closed-form law separating prefill from decode and KV-cache cost; R^2 0.969 and 11.97% MAPE over 13 dense LLMs on H100, 3.1x to 3.5x lower error than the previous best model, without per-model calibration (2 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03330
- When Every Choice Is Correct: A Causal Study of Numerical Preference in Language Models — Why a model prefers 17 when any number from 1 to 30 is valid; causal edits show later MLP computation changes how similar local contributions affect the continuation (4 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06025
- Typological Feature Induction as a Benchmark for Inductive Metalinguistic Reasoning — LLMs identify 59% to 80% of 195 languages from 16 sentence pairs, barely harmed by misaligned translations, suggesting reliance on prior knowledge; on unidentified languages open models do no better than a majority baseline (3 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04421
- Phonological Knowledge of Chinese Characters in Language Models — PinyinBench on 17 models: frontier model above 90% on homophone judgment, but 72% when only tone differs; tone is decodable in embeddings yet steers generation weakly (4 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05803
- What makes a theorem interesting? — Mathematicians' selections in formal libraries are predicted about as well by structural features (statement and proof length, subfields) as by an LLM judge, with largely shared weights across evaluators (2 Nov, single author). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02905
- Outdated numeric values from Norwegian statutes — A preregistered paired comparison of Borealis 2 and Gemma 4 on stale legal values; Gemma 4 gave outdated answers more often, with the caveat that it compares released models and cannot isolate any training step. Data and code on Zenodo and GitHub (2 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02334
- Controlling Sharpening in LLM Post-Training — Closed-form optimal policies for pass@k and max@k show learning pressure shifts toward harder prompts, and standard pass@1 objectives reach the same policies with prompt-adaptive regularisation (2 Nov). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03146
