An unnamed author writing at Mondobe argues that AI-assisted programming can offer attractive career opportunities while eroding the craft and control that made the work appealing.

The October 2 personal essay comes from someone describing life among computer-science teaching assistants and students. The author hears enthusiasm for AI jobs around them but experiences the same changes as a loss of belonging.

**Why it matters:** A manager mentoring a new developer may encounter concern that is about the work itself, rather than a prediction of unemployment. The essay gives that concern a concrete voice without presenting it as representative survey evidence.

The author objects to a future spent prompting coding tools instead of making software directly. They also question why faster development has not translated into noticeably better versions of the applications they use. Both points describe a person's experience and expectations, not an assessment of every product or programmer.

There is an argument underneath the frustration: gaining a faster way to produce code does not settle what kind of work people want to do. A developer can benefit from automation and still care about understanding the system, exercising judgment and seeing a connection between effort and result.

For a technical lead, that suggests a useful conversation about which responsibilities a junior engineer retains. Reviewing a design, explaining a failure and owning a result can matter even when another tool produces the first implementation. The essay is a reason to ask that question, not evidence that one training policy will solve it.

The author eventually imagines rebuilding the industry's institutions and finding a more settled role for AI. The closing request for a home in the industry captures the piece's main concern: whether programming remains somewhere this person wants to belong.
