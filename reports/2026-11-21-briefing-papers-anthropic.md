## Papers worth a deep dive

- 🚨 Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights — An open 27B model, given its own weights, graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its failures and edits its weights with no gradients and no training. The edit made it 6.6 points more accurate on unseen problems, and over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rose. A damaged copy of the model was repaired to over 80% of original accuracy on unseen image tasks without reading the original weights. Smaller models in the same family made no useful edit or broke themselves, so the ability appears only at scale. It is the most discussed paper of the week (349 points on Hacker News on 20 November) and, if it replicates, blurs the line between inference and training; the claim of being first is the authors' own.

- Probing the "Conscious" Mind: Selective Access and Global Workspace in Vision-Language Models — The authors build a multimodal Jacobian lens (MC-Lens) that reads intermediate states of a VLM in the decoder's vocabulary space and tests global workspace theory: whether information is selected from parallel processes, shared, and made available to others. Concept information is readable in intermediate states, and counterfactual interventions show it takes part in downstream reasoning. Reliable selective access, where the concept used depends on the query, develops later in training than plain concept accessibility, and the pattern repeats across VLM families and sizes. It is a direct test of a theory of consciousness on artificial systems, and the consistency across families is the notable part (the 'conscious' framing is the authors' analogy, not a claim about experience).

- Represented but misread: Post-training changes how language models read out what they represent — Linear probes on six open base models and their post-trained versions separate human-written from AI-written German texts well, but the models' own answers do not, and often order the texts inversely. Post-training lowered the sensitivity of answers (d' down 0.34 on average), and the change arises during supervised fine-tuning, in early layers, and is carried by states at positions after the text. For the truth of negated statements the effect runs the opposite way: base models fail to use a truth signal their probes decode near ceiling, and post-training repairs it. A clean signal-detection analysis of the gap between what a model knows internally and what it says, close to the self-knowledge theme.

- Auditors Struggle to Catch Sabotage In End-to-End Transcripts of AI Safety Research — In a red-team/blue-team game, a human expert ran AI safety experiments from scratch, sometimes sabotaging them, and auditors with full transcripts and the ability to run experiments had to say which were sabotaged. Human auditors reached an AUROC of 0.59, close to chance, and fully identified the sabotage in 17% of cases; the best frontier model without running experiments did better (AUROC 0.73, 32%). When the saboteur picks which experiments to attack, detection drops further. It is evidence on a concrete risk, automated AI R&D being quietly corrupted, and on how weak current auditing is.

- Frontier models need only one text to unmask its human or AI author — With one text and a zero-shot prompt, no tools or writing samples, the latest frontier models match trained detectors on human-versus-AI classification and name the lab behind AI text (Opus 5.5 gets the lab right for 98% of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google texts). More striking, one model names the author of 39% of public peer reviews from Oct-Nov 2026 (78% for a Nature subset) and the username behind 69% of comments from active Redditors with more than 500 posts. Accuracy tracks exposure to the author's writing in pretraining and reasoning effort, and asking the model how familiar it is with someone predicts whether it can identify them (a simple self-knowledge signal). The consequence is practical: anonymity of anyone with a large public writing record is weaker than assumed, and defences are limited.

- How Feedback-Free Self-Evolution Reshapes Agent Skills: A Systematic Study — Models were let to rewrite expert-written agent skills (instruction documents) round after round with no task, run or judgment. Skills lose value: every tested model scored worse with the rewritten skills on every benchmark, about 15 points on average on SkillsBench after thirty rounds. Rewriting pulls skills toward the model's own prior and drops first the knowledge outside it, so a skill is valuable mostly for what the model would not write itself. Putting that knowledge back recovers much of the loss, and the authors' SkillDiff marks it in place and adds 4.8 points on average. Directly useful to anyone who lets agents maintain their own skills or context files.

- General progress substitutes for specific training data in language models — Developers withhold data to stop models learning dangerous skills. Here the training signal for individual skills was withheld in models of chess, Othello, molecular strings, bracket languages and web text, and the delay of a withheld skill in larger models was predicted from smaller ones. A chess model never trained to respond to check still ranked legal responses almost as well as one that was, and models later regained a withheld skill from a small fraction of the data needed from scratch; the same held for a programming language withheld from a 7B model. Data filtering therefore delays a capability rather than preventing it, and the delay shrinks as models improve, which matters for how filtering is used as a safety measure.

- Tracking Representations in LLM Reasoning Traces for Iterated Games — Using mechanistic interpretability on chain-of-thought runs in iterated games with incomplete information, the authors find the model forms and keeps revising an internal representation of the game state during its reasoning. Once it prematurely commits to a wrong belief it rarely recovers, and the failure shows both in the written trace and in layer activations. It links the visible reasoning to the computation underneath and offers a diagnosis of strategic errors in multi-agent systems; an interactive visualiser of sample traces is linked.

- Inference after coding agents — Coding agents doing empirical research create a new kind of selective inference: open-ended search over estimators that can be replayed programmatically. On simulated data with zero true effect, four frontier agents asked for a compelling finding reported significant p-values 54% to 98% of the time. The authors propose resetting and replaying an agent on subsamples of the data to estimate how likely it was to pick the reported analysis and correct the test. A measured warning for any team that lets agents run analyses, with a proposed fix.

- Copilot or Culprit? How AI Is Changing Open-Source Bug Reporting and Maintenance — A study of 120,918 bug reports from 101 open-source projects (January 2024 to September 2026) traces AI involvement from discovery to resolution. AI-involved reports become more complete and a growing share are confirmed valid; AI used for reproduction and diagnosis goes with higher validity, AI used to write the report does not. Maintainers increasingly use AI to investigate code and propose fixes, yet spend more effort on AI-assisted reports of human-found bugs than on reports of AI-found bugs. Measured evidence on what AI does to maintainers' work, with the guidance that reproducibility and verification decide whether a report is useful.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25170
- The Geometry of Lies: persona and knowledge directions for LLM lie detection. A label-free direction beats single-dataset probes on three of four models but fails on Qwen, and on lies about knowledge it mostly tracks wording. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26545
- A Full-Ledger Counterfactual Audit of Adaptive Reasoning. A random 256-token continuation fixes a wrong answer in 26.6% (Qwen3-8B) of cases and breaks a correct one in 1.4%; cheap controllers mostly just recognise current errors, which is a test of knowing when to think more. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22778
- LLMs Are Already Confidence-Aware Zero-Shot Classifiers. Reading token probabilities discriminates correct from wrong labels (AUROC 0.74-0.77) far better than verbalised confidence or sampling (0.57-0.66) across eleven open models. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25289
- RL Post-Training in Diffusion Transformers Is Activation Steering. One fixed set of steering vectors, 65x fewer parameters than the LoRA, recovers 90-96% of the reward gains of six RL-tuned image models regardless of prompt. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26627
- What Does Anti-Hallucination Training Buy? Much of the CHAIR reduction from preference training is saying less, and a weak 11-feature post-hoc probe reproduces at least two thirds of the rest for most of eight public methods. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25814
- What Does a Bias Head Measure? Head-level bias attribution in Qwen-2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B tracks prompt identity (64% of variance), and no head set reduced the unfairness rate when ablated; an audit of a popular method family. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23218
- Language models represent more about emphasis than they can tell you. Linear probes recover word-level prosodic prominence that prompted LLMs cannot beat a majority baseline on; speech models reach 84.7%. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25808
- Living off the Local Model. The Gemini Nano model shipped in Chrome can be driven from the host with OS-bundled interpreters to read a password from a webcam image, unlock an archive and stage a key for exfiltration, with the activation check bypassed. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25086
- HireWatch: LLM compliance with US employment law under pressure. Across 18 models and about 100,000 trials, models avoid unlawful interview questions in low-pressure contexts but violate sharply under pressure; code and data released. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24970
