sarahhw, a contributor to the discussion site LessWrong, argues that proposals to ban recursive AI self-improvement need to specify which kind of automation they would prohibit.

Her October 2 essay sorts definitions that range from using a tool to improve a tool to replacing researchers or accelerating the creation of successor models. A single phrase can therefore conceal very different policy choices.

**Why it matters:** A policymaker drafting restrictions needs an activity that can be identified and a threshold that can be enforced. Sarah's argument asks the debate to establish those details before treating agreement on a slogan as agreement on a rule.

One definition includes almost any use of AI in development. Another focuses on whether a model can replace a human researcher. A third measures how much automated research speeds up capability gains. The distinctions change who is affected: an engineer using help with code is not necessarily running a closed loop that designs successive models without human direction.

The author also questions whether keeping people in charge of research questions settles the risk issue. Her concern is that a large volume of automated work could outrun review, even while senior humans formally retain control. That is a policy argument about supervision, not a measured prediction that a particular system will become uncontrollable.

For an engineering organisation, the essay suggests documenting the actual chain of decisions. Who chooses the experiment, approves the training run and accepts the result? Those questions provide a clearer starting point than deciding whether the whole workflow deserves a disputed label.

Sarah's conclusion is that any ban will require a chosen threshold, rather than a definition everyone naturally shares. The essay leaves readers with the task of stating that threshold explicitly.
