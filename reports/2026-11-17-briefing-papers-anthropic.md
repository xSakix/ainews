## Papers worth a deep dive

1. Reasoning models confabulate to defend a belief that was injected into them — An activation edit that is invisible in the prompt text, an injected 'forming-trace' of an off-topic concept, overwrites a reasoning model's belief about what the user asked. In six models the injected topic becomes the task the model reasons about, reports back and answers (quote-back returns the injected topic in 47 of 48 model-concept cells; the injected answer follows in 99% of generations on four models). Naming the true topic in a warning yields under 5% true-topic answers, and given the truth in clean tokens the model invents a story about a confused user and still answers the injection. Attention maps and circuit attributions also read as on-task. It matters because CoT monitoring and self-report are proposed as oversight tools and this is a concrete case where the account of the request is cut loose from the request. Single-author, activation-injection setting, so it shows a limit rather than a rate in the wild. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17958

2. Sandbagging detectors fail once the attacker adapts to them — The authors fine-tune small open-weight models to fail GSM8K problems they can solve, but only when an audit marker is in the prompt. Two rounds of retraining against a text-based audit cut its AUC from 0.978 to 0.560 while the marker still removes 40 to 49 points of accuracy; the attack worked in 7 of 8 retrained seeds. Activation probes flagged 99% of benign fine-tunes' examples and transferred to only 1 of 5 held-out training runs. For anyone reading an evaluation score as evidence of capability, the point is that a detector tested once against a fixed attack has not been shown to catch an adaptive one. Small models and a toy sandbagging trigger limit how far this extends to frontier systems. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14950
Authors: Bhoj Rani Soopal, Aarohi Agrawal, Josh Karthikeyan (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 11 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: no (evaluation integrity)

3. Contagious fingerprints: a model that continues another model's conversation inherits its style — Classifiers that identify which LLM wrote a text are used to audit API relays that might serve the first turns with the model you pay for and the rest with a cheaper one. After such a handoff, detection of the substitute falls from 0.97 to 0.72 (DeepSeek replacing Claude Sonnet) and from 0.87 to 0.54 for a same-vendor downgrade (Haiku); preregistered experiments trace this to imitation of the history's style. Active probes calibrated on fresh conversations also flag honest models on long contexts at rates up to 1.00. The authors propose Lineup, which ranks the audited reply against references sampled from the claimed model on the same history, reporting a 0.056 false-positive rate on two closed models. A direct consequence-side result for buyers of API access: model substitution is harder to catch than assumed. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18104
Authors: Zailong Tian (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 13 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: no (consequence: API substitution audits)

4. Can an LLM be made believably dumber? A benchmark for performance throttling — CapLadder is an IRT-calibrated suite of 1,985 items (maths, code, multiple-choice reasoning, tool use) for asking whether a model can be driven to a target score with errors that look like a naturally weaker model's. A weak-teacher LoRA 'dial' hits unseen target levels on reasoning with 1.9 points of calibration error, which prompting, temperature and weight noise cannot match. On multiple choice the dial collapses into an answer-token attractor (up to 97% of new errors on one letter, D in Qwen, A in Llama), and subtracting the estimated logit shift recovers 52 to 59% of lost accuracy. Human coders still found the reasoning errors indistinguishable from the weak reference. Relevant to sandbagging, evaluation gaming and what a benchmark score proves. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15568

5. When expert disagreement hurts: LLMs revise correct answers when told an expert disagrees — A controlled two-pass audit holds question, evidence and initial answer fixed and varies only the revision context: neutral reflection, anonymous disagreement, or disagreement attributed to a domain expert. Revision only pays if models reverse wrong answers more than a/(1-a) times as often as right ones (nine times at 90% initial accuracy). Across five models and 1,989 evidence-grounded decisions in medicine, science and law, none clears that bar under expert disagreement and accuracy falls by up to 16.1 points; the expert label raises reversals in four of five models even when the arguments are identical. A clean, quantitative account of deference to authority, a human cognitive bias, in models, and a warning for 'ask another agent to review' pipelines. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16694
Authors: Yupeng Tang, Mingfeng Lin (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 12 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes (authority bias, deference)

6. Why teams of small models barely beat one model on Fermi estimates — Using 2.51M answers from 15 open-weight models (3B to 72B) on realFP and two replication sets, the authors split each answer's log error into an item-level component shared by all draws of a model on that item and an independent residual. The shared component is a median 0.906 of the error variance, so teams of thirty cut a single agent's error by a median 1.7% (at most 5.4%) instead of the 74 to 79% that independence would give. Three rounds of debate shrink within-team dispersion 93% and leave the shared error unchanged; mixing five models of similar accuracy helps by 6.6%. All models compress estimates toward about 10^6. It explains why 'more agents' often disappoints: the agents are wrong about the same things. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16315
Authors: Carolina Fortuna, Blaz Bertalanic (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 12 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes (shared systematic error, estimation)

7. The best probe direction is not the best steering direction — Probe directions are routinely reused to steer models. In three instruction-tuned models, for truthfulness and sentiment, the best probe is not the best steering direction: steering favours directions closer to the plain mean difference, in 59 of 60 layer-model-concept combinations. Transfer across models differs too: a probe carried over by a linear map keeps 81 to 91% of above-chance performance, while the transferred mean difference keeps less of its steering effect in 11 of 12 model pairs (though nine pairs are inconclusive by the authors' own criteria). A useful caution for interpretability work that reads a direction as 'the representation' and then intervenes on it. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19499
Authors: Afshin Sadeghi, Daniel Becker (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 14 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes (representations, interpretability)

8. Can mechanistic interpretability explain why a web agent hallucinates a button? — Applied to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on live websites, a linear probe on the last prompt token predicts action hallucination before generation starts (0.80 AUC), and steering along its direction corrects 85% of hallucinations while disrupting 7% of valid actions. But the probe gives only a partial explanation: the computation is spread across components and positions and the information the agent relies on varies by example, which the authors say challenges the assumptions behind standard circuit-discovery tools. A careful partly-negative result on moving interpretability from single prompts to agent failures. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18514
Authors: Mohamed Aghzal, Mor Geva, Gregory J. Stein (affiliations not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 13 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes (interpretability)

9. When a document redefines a word, models keep giving the usual meaning, with high confidence — Questions whose answer depends on a local definition (synthetic vocabulary, commercial contracts, Reddit glossaries, a decentralized exchange's documentation) were asked to three models with and without the definition. Familiar words redefined by the source cause the most errors, models often repeat the same wrong answer, and neither repetition nor stated confidence reliably flags it. Supplying the definition fixes most synthetic errors but only about half in real contracts. For professionals using LLMs on contracts and manuals, it is a concrete case where self-consistency and confidence do not work as error detectors. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15597
Authors: Zengqing Wu, Chuan Xiao (affiliation not stated in abstract) · First submitted: 11 Nov 2026 · Artefacts: not stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes (calibration, prior knowledge vs context)

10. What moves a LoCoMo score: the judge and the answer prompt, not just the memory — LoCoMo is a widely cited long-term conversational memory benchmark. On ten conversations (1,540 questions), two answer models, two prompts and three LLM judges, changing only the judge moves a score by up to 7.2 points and only the answer prompt by up to 9.3. A blind human check of 150 answers finds the judges reliable when they agree. The evaluated coding-agent memory system, Becos, answers from a context with 90% fewer characters than the whole conversation. Relevant to anyone comparing agent-memory systems by their published LoCoMo numbers. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19199
