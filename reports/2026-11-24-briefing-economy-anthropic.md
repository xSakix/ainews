## 1. Jobs, skills and professions

- Agentic AI exposure predicts weaker hiring, mostly for non-senior jobs — A new arXiv paper (Borri, Cheremukhin, Liu, Tsyvinski; submitted 20 November, v1) is the first to link realised agent usage to job postings across the economy. It combines about two quadrillion tokens of realised AI use from OpenRouter (about 2 percent of global token consumption, 1.1 quadrillion of them agentic, more than 400 models) with the full text of 34.8 million US job postings from public firms (Lightcast). Each firm gets an "agentic beta", the co-movement of its stock return with weekly agentic token use. A one-standard-deviation higher agentic beta predicts 2.11 percent lower posting growth over six months and 3.20 percent over nine months. In an event study around the Claude Code 2.0 release, a one-standard-deviation higher pre-release exposure predicts 4.73 percent lower posting growth over the next six months. The drop is concentrated in non-senior jobs and is not statistically significant for senior jobs; broad AI usage exposure has no significant effect once agentic exposure is controlled for. Limits: this is a working paper, not peer reviewed; it measures job postings (demand), not layoffs or employment; exposure is inferred from stock-return co-movement, and the OpenRouter sample is 2 percent of global tokens. The authors validate the measure against AI mentions in filings and earnings calls. Who is affected: junior and non-senior staff at US public firms with high agent exposure · Evidence: study (working paper, public-firm postings plus token data) · Tension: firms most exposed to agents post fewer junior roles, while senior postings show no significant change. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27210

- Weak links: NBER paper says automation explains growth, but tasks still done by labour slow an AI "growth explosion" — Jones and Tonetti (NBER w36050, 23 November) run a task-based growth accounting for the US and find that capital task productivity has grown about 5 percentage points per year faster than labour task productivity; the main gain from automation is using fast-improving machines instead of slowly improving humans on more tasks. They calibrate a growth model in which goods and ideas are endogenously automated: growth accelerates and can explode, but "weak links", tasks that stay with humans, slow the acceleration. The 5-point gap is the only figure in the abstract; the explosion is a model result, not a forecast. Who is affected: workers in tasks that are hard to automate, and anyone modelling AI growth · Evidence: study (working paper, calibrated model) · Tension: none. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050

- AlphaFold2 shifted structural biologists toward deepening known proteins, not exploring new ones — Mohnen and Gans (NBER w36045, November 2026) link 530,471 proteins to the experiments run on them from 2017 to 2025 and compare protein groups with more and less confident AlphaFold2 predictions after 2021. Where predictions are confident, fewer first-ever structures appear for proteins far from any known relative, and follow-up experiments on proteins with known structures rise, most for proteins with close relatives. The authors read this as AI replacing structure-determining experiments and cheapening follow-up work, so it "deepens rather than extends" knowledge. This is a concrete case of what delegating to a model does to a profession's choices; the abstract gives no effect sizes, and it is one field. Who is affected: structural biologists and lab budgets · Evidence: study (working paper, 530,471 proteins) · Tension: a tool built to widen the frontier narrows where experiments go. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36045
