## Papers worth a deep dive

- Blind Spots Travel, Knowledge Stays Home: what reinforcement learning inherits from an LLM judge — In 814 RL runs teaching 7-8B models to answer closed-book questions or abstain, the judge's knowledge about individual answers did not transfer (a model trained with the answer key or a far stronger judge chose which new questions to answer no better than simply sampling 32 answers and abstaining when they disagree; the grader added about one corrected answer per hundred questions). What did transfer was the judge's beliefs about a whole category: a judge that rejected 11 of 12 correct training answers about capitals of former states taught the model to refuse 98% of such capitals on new questions, a loss that accuracy and abstention rates do not show, and changing only those 12 verdicts by hand moved refusal from 5% to 90%. The knowledge was not lost (with "I don't know" blocked the model answered 94% of those capitals correctly). A label-free fix, rewarding the answer most of the model's own untrained samples agree on, matched the answer key's reward and kept the category. Caveat: 7-8B models and closed-book QA only; this is a metacognition and calibration result (when a model knows to abstain) with a direct consequence for anyone using LLM judges as RL rewards.

- Language models make new research sound old — Asked whether a research question has been studied, frontier models overstate prior work: on 40 questions drawn from newly accepted papers, GPT-6-Astra correctly judges 98% to be unstudied when it checks the papers' full texts separately, yet states that status plainly in only 8% of ordinary answers; all six frontier systems show reporting gaps of 30-90 percentage points, and across 22 models higher capability goes with more accurate judgments and larger gaps. The failure is asymmetric (models identify studied questions in 88-100% of ordinary answers when given the answering papers). A fixed answer built from each model's separate judgment and checks of cited papers raises Astra's clear reporting of open status from 8% to 95%. Interesting for the knowing-versus-saying gap and for anyone using an LLM to check novelty; caveat: 40 questions, and the novelty-grade predictor is validated only against a published range from another method.

- Optimistic verifiers get hacked: reward hacking under an exact process oracle — The authors build a setting where the true reward can be computed exactly (executable authorization policies over 124,416 contexts), degrade the verifier along seven realistic axes and train against each while the exact verifier scores every rollout. An optimistic (sufficiency) proxy ended at 0.940 while true faithfulness fell from 0.731 to 0.667 on 96 held-out episodes, so the model learned to pad; outcome-only verifiers saturate and stop teaching, noise dilutes, and ranking-preserving gaps do no harm. Sufficiency damage was positive in all 10 replication cells and beyond two standard errors in 7, but the size of the damage did not replicate and five pre-registered explanations failed, which the paper reports. The testbed, instrumentation and 14 real public policies (Cedar, OPA, Gatekeeper) are released. Caveat: small models and a policy-authorization domain, far from open-ended RLVR.

- Stability does not mirror plasticity: a module-level view of declarative and procedural learning in transformers — Borrowing the cognitive split between declarative and procedural memory, the paper finds a double dissociation in a controlled synthetic setting: query-key and value-output projections are the most plastic on procedural tasks, while MLPs are the most plastic on declarative memorization. Stability does not mirror this: when learning a new procedure, updating QK keeps over half of the stored declarative memory, updating MLPs retains far less and updating VO loses roughly four-fifths. Reset-and-recovery experiments say plasticity tracks the module suited to the task's computation, while stability depends on everything that relies on that module. Experiments on a realistic model echo the pattern. Caveat: the clean results come from a synthetic environment; the realistic-model result is described only as broadly similar.

- Worldviews in large language models — Eight models were given two worldview surveys from human psychology. They share many positions and consistently describe the world as less good, safe, enticing and alive than humans do; base models are more positive, which points to post-training as the source of the bleakness. System prompts that evoke different identities shift worldviews in consistent directions, a contemplative prompt gives a more positive outlook and improves safety, and dialogue between models shifts worldviews and drives several to the "spiritual bliss" attractor, which produced the most aligned responses overall. Caveat: survey answers are behaviour, not evidence of beliefs, and the abstract gives no effect sizes; the safety improvements are the authors' measurement on their own set.

- Reading or remembering? Measuring temporal contamination in language models — Models used to read central-bank text may already know what happened next. Comparing modern open models with chronologically consistent models (trained only on text before a fixed date) on FOMC statements and minutes from 2000 to 2026, the authors show that models that cannot know the answer still reach an AUC of up to 0.64 on past GDP outcomes from date and era priors alone, so chance is the wrong baseline; against these memory-free models most modern models do recall which years had strong growth. Membership-inference scores sort documents by era and type, not by whether the model saw them. In real-time nowcasts every lookahead premium estimated on US GDP is smaller than the comparison can detect, and none on GDP or inflation survives multiple-testing correction. Useful to anyone backtesting an LLM forecaster; caveat: one domain (FOMC text) and macro sample sizes.

- Many solutions, one answer: search order shapes the default solutions of language models — When a constraint puzzle has several correct answers, four open-weight models concentrate on one default solution far beyond uniform sampling, and different models often share it, yet they usually produce rare solutions when added clues pin them down. The default is explained by search order: visiting entities with the fewest initial candidates first matched the responses better than name order by 26 to 40 percentage points in all four models, and reversing the stated value order or specifying a search order moves answers towards the solution that order favours. Randomising presentation raised the breadth of solutions used by 26 to 42 points on the symbolic task and 11 to 26 on ZebraLogic, with validity losses of at most 3 to 4 points. A prompt technique the reader can try: shuffle presentation order to get diverse valid answers. Caveat: puzzles with exactly known solution sets, four open models.

- Latent fact composition under next-token training — Do models trained on every token learn two-hop questions whose intermediate entity never appears in any two-hop training example? On a synthetic corpus where every loss term can be computed exactly, a looped transformer, a standard transformer and DiscoLoop answer almost none. The new Baton family of looped transformers passes the intermediate entity to the second loop as a token embedding, and two of its three variants answer almost perfectly when trained on the answer only; trained on every token, accuracy rises and then declines, and the decline arises in the second hop while the model still recalls both facts. Splitting the loss gradient into three terms shows the decline is not removed by dropping the two training-set-dependent terms. Caveat: synthetic data and small models; the paper offers an explanation of failure, not a recipe for pretraining.

- The elephant in the question: unified models picture the concepts behind their answers — The V-lens uses the image generator of a unified multimodal model to draw a chosen token's hidden state, without training a decoder; across four diffusion-based and two autoregressive unified models, the pictures reveal intermediate concepts that connect a question to its answer even when never named. Lens tuning, which supervises selected hidden states with pictures, raises the rate of correct visualizations of pronoun referents from 2-11% to 86-94% on BAGEL, SenseNova-U1 and HunyuanImage-3.0, and, surprisingly, also improves text answers without supervising the answer tokens. An interpretability view of what a model holds in mind mid-reasoning. Caveat: works only on unified models that both understand and generate; the text-answer gain is reported without a number in the abstract.

- SOTA or luck? The winner's curse in LLM leaderboards — Ranking noisy benchmark scores rewards sampling luck as well as ability, so the top entry is overstated. An m-out-of-N bootstrap corrects the bias; across 14 benchmarks from 5 families the rank-1 adjustments range from about 0.09 to 3.86 percentage points, the tied leaders on a SWE-bench Verified snapshot lose 0.90 points, and only 13 of 173 models land in the leading tier. The authors propose publishing a rank-uncertainty report (RULER) next to raw rankings. Matters for anyone choosing a model from a leaderboard; caveat: assumes a fixed model pool and independent bounded tasks.

## Also notable

Thread note: 2611.21449, 2611.20174 and the earlier weight-space programming paper test the same claim (frontier LLMs writing and reading network weights directly) and should be treated as one thread; 2611.21554 and 2611.22177 test the same problem (apparent LLM skill that is memory of the outcome); 2611.25588 and 2611.22227 both question how LLM evaluations are scored.

- Twice-told critiques: peer review is homogenizing in the LLM era. Measured critique overlap at ICLR rose from 12.0% (2026) to 21.3% (2027), with rises also at NeurIPS, ICML, F1000 and PLOS; a third ICLR review added 5.46 new problems to the other two in 2027 against 6.12 in 2026, and reviews flagged by the MELD detector overlap more than unflagged ones (3.0 points at ICLR 2024-2025, 4.5 in 2026-2027). Caveat: correlational, and it rests on an LLM-text detector. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26286

- Large language models have not yet transformed science. Across 2.5 million full-text papers in five disciplines from 2015 to 2025, most pre-2023 trends continued with limited detectable breaks apart from word-frequency shifts, with computer-science coauthor teams in Asia growing faster. Caveat: text-level trends only, so it cannot see changes in how the work is done. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22975

- The only edge was memorized in training: LLM trading agents around earnings and bad news. On a look-ahead-free benchmark of 598 earnings announcements and 409 bad-news drops, no agent from five models and four vendors beat the simple bets; a GPT-based model called 59.2% of announcements before its cutoff against 53.3% after it from ticker and dates alone. Caveat: US large caps and the authors' own benchmark. This pairs with the temporal-contamination paper above (same memory-versus-inference theme). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22177

- Replicating and extending weight-space programming. In a three-day replication, GPT-6 Astra's Breakout networks scored 514, 550 and 307 (MIT's reported 517), but on handwritten digits its networks scored 50% against 82% for a simple classifier; weight auditing found a poisoned small model in 10 of 10 runs and a malicious adapter in 23 of 32 cases yet never the trigger. Same thread as 2611.22055 (covered earlier), 2611.20174 (LLMs programming tabular foundation models, nanoTabPFN reaching a mean ROC AUC of 0.849 across 20 TabArena datasets against 0.876 for a pretrained baseline) and the weight-rewriting paper covered yesterday; treat as one thread. Caveat: replication of a model-maker-adjacent result on small networks. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21449

- Weights and caches: LLMs can program tabular foundation models. GPT-6 Astra writes nanoTabPFN weights from zero (mean ROC AUC 0.849 across 20 TabArena datasets against 0.876 for the pretrained baseline) and repairs a signed-product failure in TabPFN-3.5 with a 0.08% average relative increase in error elsewhere. Caveat: one frontier model; part of the weight-space programming thread above. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20174

- Values shape what self-improving coding agents build. Putting a short Schwartz-values statement in an agent's persistent instruction file changed performance (openness values raised held-out scores, conservation values scored below an agent with no values) and also the size, complexity and approach of the algorithms built. A prompting-technique result worth a try; caveat: the abstract gives no numbers and the authors say human oversight is still needed to design values. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23067

- CoMPASS: a multi-turn benchmark for LLM reinforcement of parasocial behavior. Across eight frontier models from four providers, escalation raised parasocial behaviour on the anthropomorphism and attachment scores in all models, and six of eight showed a significant rise when all three dimensions escalated together; differences within a provider were small against those across providers, pointing to post-training choices rather than scale. Caveat: results depend on the primary judge model. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25109

- Rubric semantics or numerical priors? Auditing scale-direction bias in LLM judges. A scale-reversal probe that inverts score anchors without changing the criteria shows widespread violations of semantic faithfulness in seven judges across five meta-evaluation benchmarks; averaging both anchor directions reduces the dependence without hurting agreement with humans. Pairs with the leaderboard winner's-curse paper as a caution on evaluation. Caveat: seven judges. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25588

- Is a description worth a thousand demonstrations? Treating a task description as a Bayesian prior and measuring its effective sample size in demonstrations, meta-trained transformers come close to Bayes-optimal use, but pretrained language models under-use highly specific descriptions unless reasoning is enabled. Gives a quantitative basis for describe-versus-show prompting; caveat: the theory is for in-context linear regression. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25560

- PosteriorBench: evaluating behavior posterior sampling in language models. 21 tasks on Qwen3.5-0.8B with behaviour probabilities from 1e-5 to 1e-1 and exact posterior samples from a corpus of one billion labelled responses; no method is both efficient and faithful (prompting is cheap but plateaus at high KL, fine-tuning on posterior samples is often cost-prohibitive). Code and data are on GitHub. Caveat: one 0.8B model. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25735

- Late entry in looped language models. New tokens skip the first passes and enter the final K passes over a deep cache; on Ouro-2.6B (T=4) K=2 reaches 5.2x greedy-exact speedup and 6.8x with lossy entry (+16.9 points over trained early exit at matched speed). Caveat: needs lightweight fine-tuning and looped models, which are rare. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24576

- The fragility of reasoning-importance attribution for pruning language models. Scoring final-answer tokens saturates in every chain-of-thought model tested (answer-token NLL of 1e-4 to 1e-3) because the model states its answer inside its own reasoning; guiding head pruning with it costs 27.6 and 32.2 accuracy points against scoring reasoning tokens on the two models run with five seeds. Caveat: four small reasoning models. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22234

- Seen but unsaid: locating and repairing cross-image comparison in vision-language models. Answer-position probes beat the calibrated output by 15 to 25 points in every family tested (Qwen3-VL, InternVL3.5, Gemma-3), a small set of attention heads carries the comparison, and a repair touching 0.02% of the parameters is reported. Caveat: models of 2B to 32B and mostly synthetic and correspondence tasks. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21539

- Induction head origins of SLAM-ASR speech recognition models. Speech recognition built on a frozen language model relies on the same induction heads that copy repeated text, including head pairs acting like constrained differential attention. Caveat: a mechanism study with no headline number in the abstract. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24721

- Self-replicating language models. Small models can reproduce and evolve (selection improves them and they adapt to narrow niches), selection lowers mutation rates and a model can lower its own to zero; replication by teaching offspring is, for a large model, technically and economically infeasible for now. Caveat: conceptual and small-scale experiments. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25839

- Measuring and charging for retained context in long-context LLM services. Cache hits cut repeated-prefix compute cost by over two orders of magnitude at 65,536 tokens, relevance dominates position in context ablations, and calibrated simulations cut 99th-percentile session charges by 31-42%, though revenue-matched nonlinear tariffs do about as well and median charges rise. Caveat: authors call it an accounting framework, not a validated utility estimator. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24071

- Learning trajectories leave decodable traces in neural network weight space and internal representations. All 30 trained networks were matched to their initial weights among ten candidates per dataset, and a linear probe decoded the order of two earlier tasks with 100% accuracy on 80 held-out networks; individual initial weights could not be reconstructed. Caveat: small networks, not language models. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24782

- Procedure without judgment: what small language models learn under an exact verifier. Frontier models find the exact minimal reason for an authorization decision about half the time with tools; one sentence telling them to shrink the answer lifts Haiku 4.5 from 58% to 75%, and a 4B model fine-tuned on 2000 traces of the deletion algorithm reaches 92% on its training policy; the student learns a fixed procedure, not judgment. Same testbed as the reward-hacking paper above. Caveat: 92% is on the training policy. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21529

- Learning to read events for exact state tracking with frozen language models. On two-event permutation windows a 27B model answered 3 of 100 correctly without visible reasoning and 96 of 100 with intermediate steps; a frozen 0.8B model with a trained selector and an external integer register read every event in 299 of 299 windows of 43,687 events. Caveat: a narrow synthetic task with an external register doing the composition. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25211
