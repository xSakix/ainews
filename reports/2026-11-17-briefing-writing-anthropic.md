## 1. Deep dives and technical write-ups

- vLLM on Vera Rubin: 58x the per-user throughput of GB300 for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash — The vLLM team (Inferact) reports that on one Vera Rubin NVL72 rack, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reaches 1.7M tokens/s per GPU while each user still gets 387 tokens/s (58x GB300 NVL72), 2.52M at peak throughput, and 1,810 tokens/s for a single user. The post explains where the gain comes from: kernels rewritten for Rubin's split memory and FP4 tensor cores (DEP2 prefill steps about 28% faster, decode steps about 25%), specialised low-latency decode kernels, and a rack-wide KV cache plus Engram tables shared over NVLink. Worth reading for anyone costing inference: it is a vendor-adjacent benchmark on pre-release-class hardware, so the headline ratio is the authors' own measurement and not independently reproduced. Author: vLLM team at Inferact, inference engine maintainers · Format: deep dive · Length: 19 min. Source: https://blog.vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-12-deepseek-v41-flash-vera-rubin

- Reinforcement learning on a 0.6B dense model: the rollout-to-update cycle cut from about 100 minutes to 56 seconds on TPU v7 — A Meta team co-designed vLLM, Tunix, MaxText and Pallas for RL on a Qwen3-0.6B dense policy and reports a 107x faster cycle (about 183K tokens/s) while keeping the full 8,192-token response budget. Their finding is that for small dense models, decode scheduling, synchronisation, attention geometry and KV-cache lifecycle, not the matmuls, become the first-order bottlenecks; the changes are upstreamed with a tpu-recipes recipe and flags in an appendix, so others can reproduce it. Author: Loki Chen and colleagues, Meta ASA and Ranking AI Research · Format: deep dive · Length: 30 min. Source: https://blog.vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-12-rl-small-dense-tpu-v7

- Encoder disaggregation in vLLM: serving multimodal models as separate Encoder, Prefill and Decode stages — Explains why the vision/audio encoding step has demand independent of prefill and decode, and how vLLM now splits it into separately deployed instances: metadata-only requests that reuse remote embeddings, a dedicated encoder runner with encoder-only CUDA graphs, embedding transfer via NIXL and Mooncake, shared caching and routing. Metadata-only EPD already works for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on vLLM main; it matches the E/P/D layout DeepSeek described for its own deployment. Useful for teams serving image or video workloads. Author: vLLM community (Inferact, InferMatrix, IBM, Mooncake) · Format: deep dive · Length: 15 min. Source: https://blog.vllm.ai/blog/2026-11-11-encoder-disaggregation-epd

- Windows 11 records which coding agents you run: a reverse-engineering of MissionControlAggregator.dll — The author, helped by an AI agent, found that a Windows system component matches process names (codex.exe, claude.exe, copilot.exe, agy.exe, cursor.exe, antigravity.exe, plus python, dotnet, gcc, code and others), behind a feature flag named CLICodingAgentUsage. It consumes existing kernel and WSL events, stores bit flags locally, and emits a Segments.SubCohorts event that he saw in the DiagTrack upload queue; the data is sent unless optional diagnostic data is switched off, which was on by default in his US install (AllowTelemetry 3). It is an independent teardown with screenshots and file paths, not a Microsoft statement; Microsoft has not been quoted in the piece. It reached the Hacker News front page (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524736, about 300 points). Author: Xusheng Li, independent engineer · Format: deep dive · Length: 7 min. Source: https://xusheng.dev/posts/windows-coding-agent-telemetry/

- Running coding agents without handing them your credentials: the "lend" tool — A practitioner's setup for agents on throwaway VMs while keys, the 1Password vault and logged-in gh/cloud CLIs stay on the laptop. lend forwards tool use to the laptop with per-request approval, keeps the socket stable across reconnects, keeps secrets and SSH keys out of the agent's view, uses time-limited leases, and shows an audit trail; the post also lists what it does not protect against. A concrete answer to a problem anyone running Claude Code or Codex on remote machines has. Author: Antonio, independent developer · Format: deep dive / tool write-up · Length: about 10 min. ## 2. Essays and arguments

- Anthropic cuts its vulnerability disclosure window from 90 to 30 days — Anthropic's Frontier Red Team argues that disclosure norms assumed human-speed fixes and rare independent rediscovery, and that neither holds when models find bugs and one model's find is likely to be another's. From 1 January 2027 the window is 30 days for high and critical issues, 45 for others, escalation to an external coordinator after 15 days if a maintainer is silent, and full details 30 days after a patch; the 7-day window for active exploitation is unchanged, and already-overdue reports get 30 more days. It is a lab's own policy statement and an argument for its position, and the post itself concedes shorter windows put pressure on maintainers (offering OSS Scanner and a free-access programme as mitigation). Consequence for open-source maintainers and security teams. Author: Anthropic Frontier Red Team · Format: policy essay · Length: 6 min. Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/coordinated-vulnerability-disclosure-timelines

- "Formal Verification is Not Verifiable" — Argues that testing is verifiable by a human (a bug report is a reviewable object) while formal verification hides trusted, hard-to-review parts (definitions, models, the prover) behind a strong forall claim, so "formally verified" gives less confidence than people assume. The author is optimistic about the field as AI makes it mainstream, favours shrinking the trusted base (for example proofs about compiled binaries) and wants claims phrased as "X proved to satisfy property P". It is the author's opinion; useful counterweight to the verification hype. Author: Alperen Keles, software engineer and formal-methods writer · Format: essay · Length: 8 min. Source: https://alperenkeles.com/posts/formal-verification-is-not-verifiable/

- "Your Agent Will Pick the Hackable One" — Argues that as software gets cheap to write, owners replace device firmware (the M-VAVE FM-1 $70 synth now has many AI-written custom firmwares, and the maker open-sourced its own under GPLv3 in October) and that buying agents will favour hackable hardware. A forecast from one example and forum anecdotes ("nearly all written with AI tools" is the author's reading of developer posts), not measured data. Author: Geoff Stearns, writer and developer · Format: essay · Length: about 6 min. Source: https://geoffstearns.com/blog/your-agent-will-pick-the-hackable-one/

- Maintainer thread: "proof of use" as a condition for open-source contributions — A maintainer on Lobsters proposes refusing PRs unless the contributor shows a real project, open or closed, that depends on the library, to filter drive-by AI-generated PRs that fix edge cases nobody meets. Replies so far (two comments at the time of reading) suggest hosting off GitHub or just putting the rule in CONTRIBUTING.md. A small thread but it reflects the maintainer burden that Anthropic's disclosure change also touches. Author: twp, open-source maintainer · Format: community discussion · Length: 2 min.
