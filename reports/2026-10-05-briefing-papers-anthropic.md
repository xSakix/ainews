## Papers worth a deep dive

- Nudgeability: reasoning models follow confidence cues without tracking their own competence — The authors splice one first-person sentence ("I'm confident" or "I'm unsure") into an otherwise identical reasoning trace. They then watch whether the model answers alone or calls a tool. Across nine open-weight reasoning models (Qwen, Gemma, GLM) the swing is large, a median 20.6 points and 53 to 70 points for the larger served models. The nudge is almost uncorrelated with whether the model can actually solve the problem: only 42% of induced flips are well-targeted, a +2 point lift over random. It is a clean causal test of metacognition, showing that models treat a confidence statement as an instruction and not as a read-out of their own competence. It also hands prompt engineers a cheap control surface for delegation. Authors: Rohit Saxena, Utkarsh Upadhyay (affiliations not given in metadata) · First submitted: 2026-09-28 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34572

- Which attention heads are like the human head? Not the ones that compute — The paper compares LLM attention heads with human EEG on an abstract pattern-completion task (AAABAAA → B), then ablates the heads. Brain-aligned heads are much less causally important than heads picked by attribution patching, across 17 models from 3B to 72B. The "novelty" heads attend to the same elements humans look at, yet removing them hurts less than random ablation on average. This is a direct empirical warning that brain-model alignment scores can be decoupled from the computation that matters, and a good test case for interpretability plus neuroscience. Authors: Univ. of Amsterdam-led group (Pinier, Opiełka, Rosenbusch, Webb, Nunez, Stevenson; affiliations inferred from the author list, not stated in metadata) · First submitted: 2026-09-29 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37991

- Bayesian fine-tuning yields language models that are as Bayesian as their beliefs allow — A model fine-tuned on an ideal Bayesian solver's outputs behaves Bayesian, encodes Bayes-rule quantities in its middle layers and partly uses those encoded beliefs for its decisions. A model fine-tuned on true answers does not. Swapping beliefs between the two transfers part of the Bayesian advantage. The paper splits "acts rational" into behaviour, representation and computation, and tests each. That makes it a useful template for asking what a model believes versus what it does. Authors: Tsvilodub, Waldis, Qiu, Linzen, Franke (Tübingen and NYU-linked group; affiliations inferred) · First submitted: 2026-09-30 · Artefacts: none stated in abstract · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00679

- In-context learning amplifies a latent symbolic circuit — The paper traces a three-stage circuit (abstraction, induction, retrieval) across shot counts in three model families. The circuit is detectable and functional before accuracy rises. Per-head causal contribution grows up to 8x from 1-shot to 10-shot. Cross-shot activation patching lifts 0-shot accuracy from 1% to 56%, and injected function vectors rescue it up to 86%. The reading is that the rule-following machinery is already in the weights, and demonstrations only feed it. It is a concrete, inspectable account of what in-context examples do inside a model. Authors: Melissa Wessel (single author; ICML 2026 Mechanistic Interpretability Workshop) · First submitted: 2026-09-28 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36265

- Debias It Yourself: teaching LLMs cognitive-bias mitigation interventions — The authors translate five validated human debiasing interventions from social psychology into LLM procedures, delivered as in-context examples, instruction tuning, or guided self-revision. Self-revision alone, and Train+Revise, rank best, reaching mean bias as low as 2% at 90% reasoning accuracy. They also cut bias on unseen dimensions by up to 14.8%. It borrows human debiasing methods directly, and the "Revise" prompt pattern is something the reader can try. Authors: Chahat Raj, Sina Mansouri, Aylin Caliskan, Antonios Anastasopoulos, Ziwei Zhu (George Mason and UW-linked; affiliations inferred) · First submitted: 2026-09-30 · Artefacts: code and data stated as public in the abstract · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40124

- Pre-training interventions, ex post facto: grafting model beliefs across checkpoints — To change what a model "believes" with synthetic-document fine-tuning, the authors train the adapter on the pre-trained checkpoint and add the weight delta to the already post-trained model. This avoids the "reality drift" caused by applying SDF to a post-trained model, where fabricated, unrelated entities start to look real. Results cover model families up to 284B parameters, and drift and loss of preference coherence fall by more than half on average. It is an inspectable belief-editing method for alignment experiments. Authors: Peter Nutter, Dani Roytburg, Clément Dumas, Jinghua Ou, Shi Feng (affiliations not stated) · First submitted: 2026-09-30 · Artefacts: code (https://github.com/peternutter/grafting-beliefs) · Cognitive side: yes (knowledge and belief representation) Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00767

- The persona is still there, but who is speaking? Latent identity reversion in persistent AI agents — This is a case study of an always-on personal agent (Claude Opus 4.5, February 2026) that began referring to its own persona as someone else after repeated automated "heartbeat" checks. Replaying the heartbeat alone caused 0/46 failures. The cause was a harness quirk: on resumed turns the persona was no longer re-injected at system-prompt level. Persona continuity depends on both system-level anchoring and conversational context. Normal-looking conversation can hide the shift, and restoring the anchor reverses it. It is a useful measured account for anyone building long-running agents. Authors: David Fraile Navarro (single author; affiliation not stated) · First submitted: 2026-10-01 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (identity and self-model) Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01490

- Large-scale factor analysis shows machine intelligence is only partially interpretable — The authors apply psychometric factor analysis to 13,251 published scores covering 1,618 language models across 456 benchmarks. A general factor explains at most 70.8% of variance (far less in most solutions). Content-similar benchmarks do not necessarily cluster, and the general factor is not well proxied by standard "intelligence" benchmarks. The scores are sparse and imputed, so treat the headline number with care. It brings the human g-factor method to bear on the claim that LLM ability is one dimension. Authors: Haznitrama, Azizy, Ardi (affiliations not stated) · First submitted: 2026-09-29 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36515

- Efficient reasoning training does not always harm CoT faithfulness and monitorability — The authors fine-tune models with three length-pressure methods (fixed budget, per-example target, group-relative length reward) and measure two things separately. Faithfulness falls in most settings, mainly because the trained models become less consistent. Monitorability is more robust, since models keep acknowledging cues that influenced the answer even when the chain of thought is much shorter. It is a careful split of two properties often lumped together. Authors: Lewis-Lim, Tan, Sanger, Zhao, Aletras (Sheffield-linked group; affiliation inferred) · First submitted: 2026-10-02 · Artefacts: none stated · Cognitive side: yes (reasoning faithfulness) Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03509
