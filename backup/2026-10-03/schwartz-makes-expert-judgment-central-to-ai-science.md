Matthew Schwartz, a Harvard physicist working as a visiting researcher at Anthropic, argues that useful AI science needs experts to choose which technically correct results deserve attention.

His October 1 essay describes BootLoops, his open-source toolkit for quantitative calculations. The model could move methods between disciplines, but collaborators helped turn those calculations into questions their fields actually cared about.

**Why it matters:** A scientist evaluating an agent's output has to judge both whether the answer is correct and whether the problem matters. Schwartz's account gives those judgments separate roles instead of treating a successful calculation as a complete research contribution.

He describes an iterative process: assemble tools, find problems suited to those tools, then bring in domain experts to redirect promising work. BootLoops belongs to Schwartz rather than Anthropic, despite his visiting appointment and the company's publication of the essay.

His practical advice includes inspecting plots, questioning conclusions and defining success clearly. He also describes organising separate work sessions and preserving plans in files so long calculations remain coordinated. These are his observations from using the system, not an independent evaluation of every result it produced.

The distinction matters when an organisation measures an automated research programme. Counting completed tasks rewards execution; deciding which tasks advance a field requires another form of review. A correct answer to an unimportant question can be a useful tool-building step, but it should not acquire the status of a breakthrough merely because an agent completed it.

Schwartz reports continued work with collaborators and makes the harness available for others to inspect and use. His essay ends by assigning people the conceptual work of direction and guidance, alongside the model's ability to connect methods and carry out calculations.
