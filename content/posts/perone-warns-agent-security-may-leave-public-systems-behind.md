Christian S. Perone, a machine-learning research engineer, argues that increasingly capable AI security agents could widen the gap between heavily tested systems and neglected public infrastructure.

His September 28 essay begins with a vulnerability he says he found in a Brazilian government system in 2020. He contacted the responsible agency, explained the issue and says it was fixed quickly. The story frames his concern about what happens when an automated attacker supplies the same attention to detail.

**Why it matters:** A public-sector system administrator has to protect services whether or not a frontier lab or security company chooses to test them. Perone asks the debate to include those systems, especially in countries with fewer resources.

He contrasts responsible disclosure by a person with an automated process that repeatedly searches for ways through an environment. The point of his anecdote is the human decision after discovering access: he says he avoided extracting data and used a phone call to report the flaw.

His present-day concern is an argument, not a measurement of how many government systems agents can compromise. He questions whether concentrating sophisticated testing in well-funded organisations leaves other operators facing stronger attackers without comparable defensive support.

For a security team, the useful implication is to include the response path in a threat model. Finding a flaw, deciding whether to exploit it and informing the owner are separate steps. A capability evaluation centred only on discovery can leave the latter decisions outside its frame.

Perone calls for moving beyond arguments organised around enthusiasm for technology or opposition to it. His essay returns to the possibility of a similar vulnerable system, with automated attempts against it and nobody making the disclosure call.
