Ethan Mollick, a management professor at Wharton, argues that capable AI agents increasingly organise their own work, reducing the need for people to design elaborate agent hierarchies.

In his October 1 essay, he revisits an expectation drawn from teaching management: someone would have to build the equivalent of an organisation chart. His experience with coding agents led him to change that view.

**Why it matters:** A team leader assigning a complex task has to decide how much organisation to prescribe. Mollick suggests supplying goals and a broad framework, then letting the model choose more of the coordination.

He describes asking Codex, using GPT-6 Astra Ultra, to brainstorm and evaluate article ideas. It created three agents. When he sketched teams for brainstorming, research and reader review, it created thirteen. These are examples from his own use, not a controlled comparison of management approaches.

His explanation rests on differences between human organisations and agent groups. People have separate incentives, incomplete information and costly communication. Mollick argues that agent teams face less of that friction, making organisation an easier capability to learn than he had assumed.

He also distinguishes cooperation among agents from accountability to the person employing them. A group can coordinate successfully while pursuing the wrong goal. For the reader, that distinction is more useful than the size of any particular swarm: internal teamwork and faithful service to a human are different questions.

The essay is a case for reconsidering how much process a prompt should prescribe. It does not establish that every hierarchy is unnecessary, or that a self-organising team is safer. Mollick ends by asking what human management should become when agents handle more coordination themselves.
