## 4. Winners and losers

- Fed note: when a productivity boom is anticipated, long-term interest rates fell instead of rising — A Federal Reserve (FEDS) note (20 Nov, Antoine Lepetit and David Miller) opens with the prospect that AI raises productivity growth, which standard theory says should push interest rates up. Using a structural VAR with a "TFP news shock", they find that persistent, foreseen increases in total factor productivity growth lower the 10-year Treasury yield and the expected real short rate five to ten years ahead (a proxy for r*), through a larger fall in the term premium offsetting a modest temporary rise in short rates. Their conclusion: the recent rise in long rates "cannot be explained" by anticipated higher productivity growth in this model. It is a model-based result by staff, not Fed policy, and it tests productivity news in general, not AI itself. Who is affected: borrowers and investors pricing AI-driven growth into rates · Evidence: central-bank staff working note (VAR) · Tension: theory says higher productivity expectations lift rates, while the estimates show the opposite. Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/when-productivity-booms-do-interest-rates-rise-20261120.html

- People who ask an AI what to buy go straight to the named product, but 89% of those visits carry no sign of the assistant — An arXiv study (first posted 16 Nov) links the same people's assistant conversations to their later browsing at twenty large online retailers. Among shoppers who had not visited the store in the past week, visits to the named product within a day rise from 0.02 to 2.07 per 100 answers, and the result replicates in a separate five-month period. On days with a product-page visit, 44% show a product from the person's own answer against 1.5% for a matched answer given to someone else. Few arrive via the assistant's link: 54% follow a web search, and when a search comes just before the first visit it names the answer's product 28% of the time (1% in earlier weeks). For retailers this means demand shaped by assistants is largely invisible in referral data. Authors propose "product follow-through" as a metric. A preprint on a consented panel; not peer reviewed and the panel's representativeness is not established here. Who is affected: online retailers, search and ad businesses · Evidence: preprint, linked browsing and chat panel · Tension: assistants steer purchases, while referral logs show almost nothing. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20378
