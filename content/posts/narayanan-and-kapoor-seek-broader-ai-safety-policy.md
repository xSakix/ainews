Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, Princeton AI researchers, argue that a broader safety movement should address existing systemic risks rather than organise itself entirely around predictions of AI catastrophe.

Their October 1 essay rejects a familiar choice between accepting existential-risk warnings and assuming those warnings are insincere. They propose another possibility: people can sincerely raise alarms while making arguments the authors consider mistaken.

**Why it matters:** A policymaker choosing preventive measures needs a coalition that can act on concrete hazards despite disagreements about distant outcomes. The authors argue that resilience against pandemics and major cyber failures deserves attention in its own right.

They credit safety advocates for highlighting chronic underinvestment in preparation. Their disagreement concerns the framing: they see AI as amplifying existing systemic risks and question making model alignment the organising answer to those problems.

This is a policy position, not a finding that catastrophic AI risk has been disproved. Its relevance lies in what an organisation might do while that debate continues. A programme can identify vulnerable infrastructure, assign responsibility for prevention and evaluate the adequacy of its response without first resolving every forecast about future models.

The essay also raises a coalition question. Agreement on one dramatic scenario is a narrow foundation for cooperation; agreement on a set of protective actions can accommodate people with different reasons for supporting them. For a technical reader, separating those two kinds of agreement clarifies what a policy proposal actually asks its participants to accept.

Narayanan and Kapoor's argument is for taking safety concerns seriously while challenging claims they find unsound. They frame the choice as whether the movement makes room for that disagreement or defines membership through a particular account of existential risk.
