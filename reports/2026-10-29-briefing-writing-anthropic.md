## Deep dives and technical write-ups

- Hybrid models can hide reasoning in their RNN state — Divyansh Agrawal fine-tuned OLMo-Hybrid-7B, Qwen 3.5 4B and a dense OLMo-3-7B control to answer a light-switch parity task with no written reasoning. OLMo-Hybrid (Gated DeltaNet write strength beta allowed up to 2) learned silent state tracking that held at twice the training length (0.89 at 128 flips, 6 seeds), while Qwen 3.5 (beta capped at 1), the dense control and OLMo-Hybrid with beta forced to 1 all fell to chance (about 0.52-0.55). For anyone who relies on chain-of-thought monitoring for safety, the claim is that one architectural number decides whether a model can reason where the monitor cannot see; this is a single-author, small-task result and the author notes a competing paper (Steifer 2026) showing capped hybrids can use attention instead. Code and raw results are on GitHub. Author: Divyansh Agrawal, independent researcher · Format: deep dive · Length: about 9 min. Source: https://divagr.com/blog/hybrid-models-can-hide-reasoning-in-their-rnn-state

- Qwenputer: a RISC-V CPU compiled into Qwen3.5's weights — Without any training, a compiler sets the weights of a stock Qwen 3.5 4B so that its Gated DeltaNet layers act as exact RAM (gate 1, write strength 1, one-hot keys) and a RISC-V CPU runs inside the forward pass, with no tool call. On exact arithmetic it scores 97.5-100% on DeepMind sets and 100% on NUPA long integers (base 48.6%), matching a Python tool; about 230,000 output positions replayed against a reference machine showed zero mismatches. Costs: checkpoint 18.8 GB (about double), needs a small driver, a few decoded tokens per instruction; an experimental in-model file store recalls changed facts 90% of the time versus 30% when old chats are pasted back. It is an author's own benchmark and was on the Hacker News front page on 29 Oct. Author: Divyansh Agrawal, independent researcher · Format: deep dive · Length: about 9 min. Source: https://divagr.com/blog/qwenputer

- DAYJOB: Tax, 50 professional tax assignments for AI agents — Surge AI (a data vendor, so read as vendor evidence) built 50 environments with 1,789 files in which an agent must find issues the client did not raise. Best pass rate at a 95% rubric threshold is 10.8% (Claude Sonnet 5.5), then Claude Fable 5.1 at 9.6% and Claude Opus 5.5 at 9.2%; the case study shows GPT-6 Astra missing a 2027 home-sale eligibility deadline in all five attempts while Opus found it in all five. The gap between fluent tax answers and work a professional would sign is the number to remember for anyone whose job is review-heavy. Author: Surge AI research team · Format: benchmark investigation · Length: about 12 min. Source: https://surgehq.ai/blog/dayjob-tax

- From Tokens to Bytes I: scaling hierarchical networks (H-Nets) — Cartesia reports scaling ladders for models that learn their own chunking from raw bytes instead of using a tokenizer: 1-stage H-Nets give up to 3-5x compute efficiency over BPE baselines, and a 2-stage H-Net is said to beat a matched open model with 10x less compute and data, with in-context tokenization behaviour seen in analysis. These are the lab's own results with a part II promised; worth reading for the tokenizer-free argument and for what the learned chunks look like. Author: Cartesia Research · Format: deep dive · Length: about 30 min. Source: https://www.cartesia.ai/blog/from-tokens-to-bytes-part-1

- Why is this fingerprinting script creating bioweapons? — While crawling 10,000 sites for canvas fingerprints, the author found a script (servicepipe.tech) packed with lethal-dose function names and a hidden Unicode-tag payload that decodes to a fake "UNRESTRICTED mode" jailbreak prompt, apparently planted so that LLM-assisted deobfuscation trips model safeguards. GPT-6 Astra refused to deobfuscate it, even with the Daybreak Blue toggle, while GLM-5.3 complied; the script also contains Node-gated code that targets .env and .npmrc files. A concrete account of safety refusals becoming an anti-analysis defence for malware. Author: veritas (nullpt.rs), security researcher · Format: post-mortem / reverse-engineering write-up · Length: about 10 min. Source: https://nullpt.rs/fingerprinting-bioweapons

- The Benchmarking Crisis — Database Architects argues that AI code generation has broken ClickBench: tuned engines such as pgrust group strings by hash only, cache repeated predicates and exploit a LIMIT without ORDER BY, which is fast but can return wrong results. Its prescription is ad-hoc queries, an update stream and systems older than the benchmark; even a 10,000-query benchmark like their own SQLStorm is no longer a defence. A useful engineering case of Goodhart's law with an AI optimiser. Author: Database Architects blog (academic database researchers) · Format: essay with examples · Length: about 5 min. Source: http://databasearchitects.blogspot.com/2026/10/the-benchmarking-crisis.html

Epoch states that customs data closely match its chip-sales estimates and lists limitations. Method and data are public, which makes it the reference for who actually has the compute. Author: Lucio Melito and Isabel Juniewicz, Epoch AI · Format: data explorer with write-up · Length: about 8 min. Source: https://epoch.ai/latest/introducing-the-ai-chip-imports-explorer

## Essays and arguments

- Artificial Analysis v5 and the benchmarker's dilemma — Nathan Lambert argues that single-number intelligence indices are forced into trade-offs, that v5 (25% each for knowledge work, coding, science, general; private prompts) widens the visible gap between closed models and the best open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, and that moving to private benchmarks removes the community's ability to find benchmark bugs. He also claims the closed labs stopped optimising for these indices long ago, which is his opinion and not shown in the piece. Author: Nathan Lambert, researcher and Interconnects author · Format: essay · Length: about 10 min. Source: https://www.interconnects.ai/p/artificial-analysis-v5-and-the-benchmarkers

- Here Be Monsters: what do you still need to know when agents do the work? — Adrian Quiroga starts from a CI failure caused by Codex writing whole components on single lines (which turned out correct), and argues that owning a system will depend less on having written it and more on knowing enough to notice when something is wrong and diving deep just in time. It is a practitioner's opinion built on one anecdote and an extended metaphor, but it frames the skills question that matters for software careers. Author: Adrian Quiroga, software engineer · Format: essay · Length: about 15 min. Source: https://blog.adrq.dev/here-be-monsters-what-do-you-still-need-to-know-when-agents-do-the-work
